Feature · Politics and the Pendulum
Each Swing Nudges Forward, Until Someone Ignores the Norms
John Day45:29–47:56
The graphic shows a typical political cycle we experience time and again. People become disillusioned, impatient or upset with current policy. Then comes "time for a change," with promises to shift direction, say from conservative to liberal, and then you swing back. Democrats need to be very aware that, even after this tremendous victory, the pendulum will start moving back within a short time.

But the pendulum's position isn't fixed. With each swing you go back a little, but you nudge forward a little, and that's how government makes progress. Think of civil rights and women's rights: for a long time we went back and forth, and we've made significant progress in 50 years. On the surface it's not a bad process. So the question is: in this last swing, when Trump came back, what went wrong?
John Day47:56–48:54

That's different from politicians in the past.

Feature · The Victory Speeches
A Shepherd Boy, a Giant, and an Echo of the Second Inaugural
George & Sally60:15–60:36
George: To his credit, John identified Talarico back in the winter, and he really is a player. Sally: He's beautiful, and his opponent was so awful. Thank God he won. Fran: If it had been John Cornyn, there would have been a big question.
James Talarico60:56–61:45 · Audio clip
In his victory speech, Talarico retold the story from Scripture of a shepherd boy facing a giant. For 40 days Goliath taunts; on the day of the fight he arrives in full armor, with so many weapons he needs someone else to carry his shield. This race, Talarico said, went up against its own Goliaths: the richest man in the world, the most powerful man in the world, and the entire Washington establishment. David wasn't as strong and didn't have as many weapons, but he had hope, and he had faith.
John Day63:03–66:07
When I heard the speech, it sounded familiar. It carried many of the same biblical allusions as Lincoln's Second Inaugural, so I was compelled to compare the two. Talarico's ability to communicate and motivate is extraordinary. He won by double what the pollsters predicted, against a formidable Texas establishment. That Texas is now blue is an amazing point to reckon with.

Both men are consummate storytellers. Lincoln used what critics called folksy tales to make his points, and Talarico does the same. Both draw on Christian scripture. The difference: Lincoln could count on an audience steeped in the King James Bible. Talarico is savvy enough to know he speaks to a very diverse audience, so instead of quoting scripture exactly, he explains and translates it so that people who aren't Christian can follow. He does it very convincingly. The full comparison is on the portal.
Sally Fleschner & George66:07–66:51
Sally: And El-Sayed, on how it will feel when Vance has to swear him in as senator. George: He called it ironic. Sally: I'd call it sweet revenge. George: He also said that Trump, who deliberately calls him Muhammad, can't pronounce his name, so he'll make it easy for him: just call me Senator. Sally: Or sir.
"He doesn't quote things exactly from Scripture, but rather he explains and translates." John Day, on Talarico and Lincoln
Background
Lincoln's Second Inaugural (March 4, 1865) is barely 700 words, yet it cites or echoes scripture repeatedly, including "judge not, that we be not judged," and closes with "with malice toward none, with charity for all." The David and Goliath story is from 1 Samuel 17, where Goliath challenges Israel for forty days.
Two Orators
  • Talarico's Goliaths: the richest man, the most powerful man, and Washington
  • He won by roughly double the predicted margin, turning Texas blue
  • Shared tools: storytelling and scripture
  • Lincoln quoted for a King James audience; Talarico translates for a diverse one
  • El-Sayed to Trump: just call me Senator

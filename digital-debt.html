The Digital Debt of Fast and Loose Comes Due

For forty years the software industry shipped first and secured later. Now A.I. is reading through the old code, finding the cracks, and the rush to patch them is knocking systems offline.

The story in brief

In the past two weeks, Americans have watched bank transactions stall, Microsoft and Apple services stumble, a classified N.S.A. network go dark for days, and all commercial air traffic in the country briefly grounded. Rumors of a coordinated cyberattack spread quickly online.

Officials say they have no evidence of malicious hacking. For several of the outages (though not the air grounding), they point to something less dramatic: organizations racing to install security fixes, and the fixes themselves going wrong.

Behind the rush is artificial intelligence. New models can comb through aging code and find flaws, sometimes building a working proof of attack in hours, that once took elite teams months. Defenders, fearing attackers have the same tools, are patching at a pace that produces messier updates.

Security insiders took to calling last month "Black October." The reporters, drawing on intelligence officials, engineers and A.I. experts, suggest it is no blip: the next year or two may be the most perilous stretch.

How the debt was run up

"Technical debt" is the security and quality shortcuts that pile up when speed wins over care. The article traces a three-stage reckoning.

I.

Borrow against the future

Competition rewarded getting products to market first. Security added friction, so it waited. One SentinelOne researcher described an industry that pays for releases but not for upkeep, running equipment until the wheels come off.

II.

The auditor arrives

Cyber-focused A.I. models make old, hidden holes easy to spot. Bug hunters are racing to find them, and companies are turning A.I. on their own systems first. The same researcher likened it to picking up brittle code and shaking it.

III.

Payment by disruption

The flood of discoveries forces hurried patches, and hurried patches break things. Many of the recent outages trace to fixes, not attacks, with A.I.-found flaws behind some of them, including the N.S.A.'s.

Why it frightens people: from the outside, an ordinary person cannot tell a malicious hack (could a retirement account be emptied?) from a clumsy update to antiquated software. That uncertainty is what fed the viral rumors.

The real danger is still the old, unlocked door

For all the A.I. headlines, most successful attacks still exploit problems that were known, and fixable, long ago.

1 in 1,400

The gap between bugs found and bugs actually used tells the story: attackers mostly don't need new holes.

  • 3–6 years old. Microsoft's security chief for customer trust says the five bugs hackers used most this past year all had patches available that long. None were A.I.-driven.
  • Default passwords. Iran's campaign against U.S. municipal water systems this summer relied largely on weak or factory-default passwords.
  • Months-old patch, never installed. A criminal group reached sensitive F.B.I. personnel files through Oracle software left unpatched.
  • Unsophisticated. Recent attacks on European refrigeration systems appear similarly basic.

Case study: one font, the keys to the kingdom

A previously unreported flaw, found with A.I., shows how deep the old code runs.

38 years

Age of Ghostscript, image-conversion software quietly built into document and image processing across the internet.

Researcher Paulos Yibelo, founder of a company called PWN AI, used A.I. to methodically probe what happens behind the scenes when people upload files to online services. The flaw he found, dubbed Upload2Shell, could have let an attacker into Google, Zoom and potentially thousands of other companies' systems. He reported it to Artifex, Ghostscript's developer, and to affected firms, which issued patches.

Fewer hands for a bigger job

Just as the bug count explodes, the people who understand old systems are thinning out.

Layoffs

Major tech companies have cut staff this year, partly to replace workers with A.I., losing the institutional memory of how their legacy code was built.

Lagging hiring

New LinkedIn data reviewed by The Times shows U.S. cybersecurity hiring underperforming hiring overall, despite record incidents.

Experts needed

LinkedIn's security chief, Lea Kissner, argues that because no one yet knows the best way to do security amid such rapid change, curious human problem-solvers matter more than ever.

After the reckoning: two forecasts

Experts agree the next year or two will be rough. They disagree about what comes after.

The optimists · Rob Joyce, former top N.S.A. cyber official

A forest fire that clears the ground

A.I. isn't inventing new ways to hack; it is forcing the fixing of old problems. Once the brittle legacy code burns away, what grows back will be stronger and more capable. Some predict A.I. will ultimately erase technical debt altogether.

"We've got to live through this era where stuff's on fire."Rob Joyce, keynote at Cyberwarcon

The skeptics · Paulos Yibelo, PWN AI

An endless cat-and-mouse game

Each new generation of models will be stronger and will keep finding new flaws, possibly even in code written by earlier generations of A.I. On this view there is no equilibrium, just a permanent cycle of discovery and patching that threatens the networks modern life depends on.

Gen. Paul Nakasone (ret.)Former N.S.A. and Cyber Command head, now an OpenAI board member, calls this week the new normal and A.I. the most disruptive technology in history, demanding a new approach to resilience.
Sens. Cantwell (D) & Rounds (R)In a bipartisan letter to national intelligence director Jay Clayton, they warned that A.I.-enabled threats are outpacing U.S. defenses and that individual companies can't solve an economy-wide problem alone.

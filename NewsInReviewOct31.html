News in Review

Eve of the Decisive Battle
Week ending October 31, 2026 · Full Analysis

With Election Day only three days—.273 Scaramuccis—away, this edition reviews how we got here, assesses the prospects for the midterms, and considers what might be a tumultuous aftermath.

Preparing the Battlefield: Redrawing the Maps

In July of last year, Trump called on Texas to gerrymander as many House seats as possible. Voluntary mid-cycle redistricting had happened only twice nationwide since 1970. A month later, Texas Republicans complied with a map projected to grow their delegation edge from 25–13 to 30–8—so contorted that one can apparently drive through five congressional districts on Austin's north side in ten minutes. California quickly answered with a map designed to gain Democrats five seats, neutralizing the Texas effort.

North Carolina and Ohio followed with maps worth 1 and 2 additional Republican seats, offset by a non-partisan Utah redistricting likely to gain Democrats one. Other attempts were stopped by legislatures or courts, leaving Republicans with a modest 2-seat gain. Then in April, the Supreme Court's Callais decision gutted the last remaining section of the Voting Rights Act, facilitating 7 more gerrymandered seats in the Deep South and lifting the expected Republican gain to 9. In an unfavorable environment, though, some gerrymanders risk becoming "dummymanders"—particularly in south Texas, where Democrats are competitive in 3 of the 4 races.

The Primaries

The primary season ran from early March to mid-September, when Delaware—the First State—ironically held the last one.

Democrats: After Zohran Mamdani's stunning NYC mayoral win last year, there was concern that DSA victories would yield uncompetitive nominees. In the event, only 7 of 25 DSA House candidates prevailed, and only Donavan McKinney in MI-13 won in a district expected to be competitive. In the Senate, DSA member Angie Nixon won in Florida, where Democrats were underdogs anyway, and DSA-adjacent Abdul El-Sayed prevailed in Michigan. Across the seven Senate battlegrounds, Mary Peltola (AK), Josh Turek (IA), Adam Hamilton (KS), Sherrod Brown (OH) and James Talarico (TX) are good to excellent candidates. In Maine, Troy Jackson stepped in for the far more charismatic Graham Platner and is running a competitive race; in Michigan, it is not clear that a more mainstream nominee would have fared better against Mike Rogers than El-Sayed, who leads in the polls.

Republicans: Trump-endorsed candidates—including 173 of 179 incumbents—won 214 of 218 House primaries, and most of the 41 non-incumbents he backed were MAGA loyalists. His endorsement propelled the deeply flawed Ken Paxton past incumbent John Cornyn in the Texas runoff, turning a likely Republican hold into a hot contest. In South Carolina, he impulsively recruited the unqualified Darline Graham to replace her brother and backed her over Freedom Caucus vice-chair Ralph Norman, turning an easy win into a somewhat competitive race against Annie Andrews that is, at minimum, drawing Republican resources.

Fueling the Voter-Fraud Narrative

In a July national address, Trump reiterated that the 2020 election was stolen, cited Chinese interference, called voting machines "extremely exposed to attacks," claimed 250,000 non-citizens were registered in CA, NJ, NV and PA, and urged passage of the SAVE Act. He cited 15,900 non-citizens on Nevada's rolls; the state found only 185 warranting review—all of them citizens.

In March, Trump issued an executive order directing the USPS to send mail ballots only to voters confirmed in the highly incomplete SAVE database. On September 14, the Supreme Court upheld lower-court rulings barring the USPS rules for the midterms. While no definitive study shows which party would benefit from curbing mail voting, 60% of party-designated mail ballot requests this year came from Democrats. As of Friday, CNN counts 48.6 million early votes—doubling over the past week and split evenly between in-person and mail—versus 46 million in the 2022 midterms and 86 million in 2024.

In recent weeks, the DOJ sent Los Angeles County a list of 20,000 "likely" non-citizen registrants and demanded a review within 48 hours. When officials noted that many were citizens and refused, the DOJ sought an emergency order to set aside those voters' ballots. A federal judge refused, saying it would "wreak havoc" with the midterms; the DOJ appealed, opened a criminal investigation, and subpoenaed election officials across the state. It has since sent Washington a list of 400 names, after earlier lists to NJ, NV and PA. Late Friday, the appeals court declined to overturn the ban on sequestering the 20,000 ballots.

States administer elections and maintain their rolls; they need not justify including voters. The administration surely knows this, and much of this is theater serving as pretext for interference on and after Election Day. Heather Cox Richardson reported seeing "StopFraud: Vote Republican" signs as she left Maine.

Campaign Overview

Republicans launched their national campaign at an early-September midterm convention in Dallas that both parties dubbed a "Trump-a-Palooza." Trump dominated and urged voters to "pretend I'm on the ballot"—a sentiment Democrats heartily endorsed. Only Fox News carried it among the five major networks, and ratings were no better than its usual programming. Afterward, the Democratic generic-ballot lead rose 3–4 points, Ken Paxton confided that it undermined his campaign, and an anonymous Republican strategist said it did nothing for independents.

Largely thanks to Elon Musk's $445 million so far, Republicans have outspent Democrats by about 50% on Senate ads, with a smaller gap in House races. Democrats have (mostly) unified behind their Senate and gubernatorial nominees and deployed Barack Obama as their closer; he opened by citing the administration's "incompetence, the likes of which we have not seen in modern times," and then got under Trump's skin with his Cheesecake Factory jab. Trump has been ubiquitous on the trail, mostly in red states; after initially cancelling Iowa and Kansas appearances in favor of TV, he has reinstated them.

The House

The final Cook and Sabato ratings both yield a 230D–205R split under this newsletter's allocation percentages—a 15-seat Democratic pick-up. RealClear Polling ratings produce 237D–198R. Kalshi and Polymarket both give Democrats a 93% chance of winning the House and forecast 240D–195R.

The generic ballot stands at D +8.9% per the Silver Bulletin, and between 8 and 9 points in most sources. In 2018 the generic ballot was D +8.0% and the result D +8.6%; in 2022 the out-party's generic ballot was R +0.7% and the result R +2.7%. A national environment near D +8 or better augurs well for Democrats.

Alaska: The race was roiled by disclosure that Peltola's campaign recruited a Daniel J. Sullivan to run as a Republican, possibly siphoning votes from incumbent Daniel S. Sullivan. Even before this surfaced, the DOJ sued to block certification if Peltola wins by less than Daniel J.'s vote total—a suit with no basis in law, since the state runs the election, the Alaska Supreme Court had already allowed the name on the ballot, and ranked-choice voting makes any effect de minimis. The Alaska Department of Law just affirmed the court's decision should stand. Peltola denies knowledge of the stratagem; it is a bad look, though such hardball is not uncommon, and Peltola herself was reportedly the victim of a similar scheme in 2024. Trump has called on her to withdraw and threatened legal action, and Governor Mike Dunleavy ordered an investigation—performative politics that hands Trump another pretext to contest results.

Kansas: As Adam Hamilton surged to a 4-point lead in the latest CNN/SSRS poll, Trump called him a "pervert" who "openly mocks Jesus," and this week a "radical left guy" whose church is "all in turmoil."

Maine: CNN/SSRS gives Troy Jackson a 49–45 lead. Susan Collins, who had earlier alluded to Jackson's partner being his second cousin, felt compelled to disavow a crude Senate Leadership Conference ad on the subject.

Michigan: The same poll shows El-Sayed surging to a 49–42 lead. On Meet the Press, Mike Rogers kept tying his opponent to the Muslim Brotherhood and did not disavow Trump's inflammatory attack on him. Kristen Welker noted El-Sayed has denounced the group and denied any ties—then put on screen Rogers's own 2012 quote about working with moderate Brotherhood members. Separately, primary rival Haley Stevens demanded an apology over an outside group's ad about Israel before campaigning with El-Sayed, prompting Mallory McMorrow to say she had "no idea what the hell Haley Stevens is doing."

Ohio: After praising incumbent Jon Husted's physique, Trump said that if Husted wins, he would consider putting the Space Academy in Ohio.

Texas: James Talarico told a rally that Elon Musk wants Texans' land, money, water and tax dollars, and that Paxton "doesn't serve us; he serves himself." Paxton continues to call Talarico "low-T" and attack him over trans athletes. CNN/SSRS has Talarico up 49–43.

"Money can only propel a campaign so far when its candidate's flaws act as an anchor." Charlie Cook, Cook Political Report

The forecast: Betting markets give Democrats a 70% chance of Senate control, and applying 100%/85%/65%/50% probabilities to the combined Cook, Sabato and RCP ratings yields 51D–49R. All seven races are within or at the margin of error, and the new CNN poll may be an outlier. Democrats have a reasonable chance in Kansas (Roger Marshall is a weak candidate), Michigan, and Ohio, while Ashley Hinson, a fairly strong candidate, should prevail in Iowa—bringing the tally to 50D–47R. That leaves three worries: Alaska because of the dual-Sullivan affair, Maine because Collins has consistently outperformed her polls, and Texas because the state controls the process and has long been the Democrats' white whale. Winning two of the three would yield 52D–48R—but with five of seven close races already awarded to Democrats, 51–49 or worse is equally plausible, especially if targeted voter suppression proves effective. Whatever the margin, expect Trump to try to overturn it with bogus fraud claims.

North Carolina has no governor's race this year; Democrat Josh Stein is the incumbent, though the Republican legislature has significantly curtailed his power. Iowa Republican Zach Lahn, notably, voted in Kansas in 2018, 2020 and 2022, and from Kansas by mail in 2024.

Among the seven presidential battlegrounds, four governorships appear securely Democratic, one is likely D, and two are toss-ups—a favorable position two years before the presidential election. Georgia holds the only battleground trifecta (Republican), which a Lance-Bottoms win would break; Democrats have shots at trifectas in AZ, MI, NV, PA and WI. Today's count is 23 R and 16 D trifectas with 11 divided states. Beyond swapping trifectas in Iowa and Kansas, Republicans have no new possibilities; Democrats could add up to six (the five battlegrounds plus Minnesota) for a 22–20 edge.

Day of the Decisive Battle

The administration will likely do what it can to suppress votes in Democratic-leaning precincts and interfere with counting. Asked by Time in September to rule out a national emergency or ICE agents at the polls, Trump said he didn't "rule anything out." What to watch for:

  • The DOJ says it will send 1,000 election observers to polls in all 50 states.
  • Armed federal agents are barred from polling places but not from congregating nearby to intimidate; ICE could also launch neighborhood operations to keep immigrants and people of color indoors.
  • Republican Party officials may appear near or inside polling places.
  • Interference with the transfer of ballots—especially mail ballots—to central tabulation sites.
  • Federal suits to stop voting by those in line at closing, or during hours extended for technical problems.
  • Amid debunked fraud claims like 2020's, a national emergency declaration to impound ballots or voting machines.

Norm Eisen of Democracy Defenders Action, recalling the May seizure of Fulton County's 2020 election materials, warned that defenders must be ready to be in court "literally in minutes."

Election Aftermath

As votes are tallied centrally, federal, party and allied outside presence could deter counting, as in 2020—with mail ballots especially vulnerable—and a national emergency could again be invoked to seize ballots or machines.

After counting, federal interference with county and state certification could delay the process. The DOJ's Thursday suit to block certification of a narrow Peltola win in Alaska has virtually no legal support but may presage similar challenges elsewhere; if it fails, Alaska's Lieutenant Governor could be pressed to delay or deny certification.

Finally, the new House—presumably Democrat-led—will be seated January 3, 2027, and the Constitution makes each chamber "the Judge of the Elections, Returns and Qualifications of its own Members." Even so, Speaker Mike Johnson could in theory replace the House clerk with a Trump loyalist willing to exclude some Democrats from the Speaker vote and preserve a Republican majority. A longshot—but few could have imagined January 6, 2021, either.

Section 1 of 7 | Treasury Bonds Basics

What Are U.S. Treasury Bonds?

A Treasury bond is a debt security issued by the U.S. Department of the Treasury. When you buy a Treasury bond, you are lending money to the federal government. The government promises to pay you back with interest. Click on each topic below to explore the details.

The Basic Concept: A Formal IOU
How Treasury bonds work and why they're safe
▼

Think of a Treasury bond as a formal IOU. The government needs cash, so it borrows from investors. Those investors receive guaranteed payments, backed by the full faith and credit of the United States government.

When you buy a Treasury bond, you receive:

  • Regular interest payments (called coupon payments) during the life of the bond
  • Full principal repayment (the original amount you lent) on the maturity date
  • Guaranteed safety — backed by the U.S. government's ability to tax and print currency

Why are Treasuries so safe? The U.S. government has never defaulted on its obligations in over 200 years. Default risk is virtually zero, as the government controls its own currency and can always pay what it owes. This makes Treasuries the safest investment in the world.

Three Main Types of U.S. Treasuries
T-Bills, T-Notes, and T-Bonds — what's the difference?
▼

Treasury Bills (T-Bills)

  • Mature in less than one year (typically 4 weeks, 13 weeks, or 26 weeks)
  • No coupon payments — you buy at a discount and receive full par value at maturity
  • Lowest interest rates because they have shortest duration
  • Most liquid Treasury instrument; used by money market funds

Treasury Notes (T-Notes)

  • Mature in 2 to 10 years (most commonly 2, 5, and 10-year notes)
  • Pay semi-annual coupon payments during the holding period
  • Medium-range interest rates
  • Most popular with individual investors; widely traded

Treasury Bonds (T-Bonds)

  • Mature in 20 to 30 years (longest duration)
  • Pay semi-annual coupon payments over the entire 20-30 year period
  • Highest interest rates due to long duration risk
  • Most volatile in price; attractive to long-term investors
Secondary Market & Market Size
Buying and selling before maturity
▼

What is the Secondary Market? All three types of Treasury securities are traded in the secondary market after they're first issued. This means you don't have to hold a bond until maturity—existing bonds can be bought and sold before their maturity date.

Key characteristics:

  • The Treasury market is the largest and most liquid debt market in the world
  • Over $27 trillion[2] in outstanding Treasury securities
  • Trades continuously—you can buy or sell any time the market is open
  • Prices fluctuate based on supply/demand, interest rates, and economic conditions
  • Bid-ask spreads are very tight (narrow profit margins for traders)

Why does this matter? The secondary market allows investors to exit positions early if needed, lock in gains, or adjust portfolio allocations. The massive size and liquidity ensure that you can always find a buyer or seller.

How Bonds Work: The Mechanics

Bond mechanics are straightforward in principle but subtle in practice. When the Treasury issues a new bond, it specifies four key details. Click on each to learn more.

Par Value (Face Value)
The amount you get back at maturity
▼

The par value is the face value or principal of the bond—the amount the government promises to repay you on the maturity date.

Typical par values: $100, $1,000, or $10,000 (varies by bond and investor type)

Example: If you buy a Treasury bond with a par value of $1,000, the government will repay you exactly $1,000 at maturity, regardless of what you paid for the bond in the secondary market.

Important note: A bond's market price can be higher or lower than par value (more on this later), but the government always repays the par value at maturity.

Coupon Rate
The annual interest rate paid on the bond
▼

The coupon rate is the annual interest rate the government pays on the bond, expressed as a percentage of par value.

How it works:

  • A 4% coupon rate on a $1,000 par bond = $40 per year in interest
  • Treasury Notes typically pay coupons semi-annually ($20 every 6 months, in this example)
  • Treasury Bills pay no coupon; you profit from the discount instead
  • Longer-duration bonds have higher coupon rates to compensate for duration risk

Why does the coupon matter? It determines your annual income stream. If you hold the bond to maturity, you know exactly how much income you'll receive. If you sell before maturity, the coupon influences the bond's market value.

Maturity & Issue Dates
When the bond is issued and when it matures
▼

Issue Date: The date the Treasury first sells the bond to the public. On this date, the coupon clock starts ticking.

Maturity Date: The date the government repays the full principal (par value) plus the final coupon payment. After maturity, the bond ceases to exist.

Duration: The time between issue and maturity. This determines which type of security it is:

  • Less than 1 year = Treasury Bill
  • 2-10 years = Treasury Note
  • 20-30 years = Treasury Bond

Why it matters: Duration determines interest rate sensitivity. Longer-maturity bonds are more sensitive to interest rate changes (their prices swing more wildly).

Bond Trading & Pricing Example
What happens when you buy/sell before maturity
▼

A Concrete Example:

You buy a 10-year Treasury Note with:

  • Par value: $1,000
  • Coupon rate: 4%
  • Semi-annual payments: $20 every 6 months

If you hold to maturity: You receive $20 every 6 months for 10 years (20 payments total), plus the final $1,000 principal. Your math is simple and guaranteed.

If you sell before maturity: The bond's market price changes based on:

  • Interest rate movements (rates up → price down; rates down → price up)
  • Time remaining to maturity
  • Supply and demand in the secondary market
  • Economic outlook and credit risk perceptions

Key takeaway: Bonds are continuously traded, and their prices fluctuate. A bond's market price can be higher or lower than par value, but the government always repays par at maturity.

Section 2 of 7 | Yield Curves & Interest Rates

Interest Rates: Short-Term vs. Long-Term

The Treasury market reveals two distinct interest rate environments operating simultaneously. Short-term rates (for T-Bills and short-dated notes) respond primarily to the Federal Reserve's current policy decisions and near-term economic expectations. Long-term rates (for 10-year and 30-year bonds) reflect investors' long-run economic outlook, inflation expectations, and risk premiums.

Normally, long-term rates are higher than short-term rates because investors demand additional compensation for lending money over a longer period. This creates an upward-sloping "yield curve." However, when economic uncertainty spikes or when the Fed raises rates aggressively to fight inflation, the curve can temporarily invert—meaning short-term rates exceed long-term rates. This inversion often signals recession fears.[1]

Key Insight: The yield curve is one of the most powerful forecasting tools in economics. When it inverts, recessions typically follow within 12-18 months.[9]

What Does the August 2026 Curve Actually Look Like?

The three charts above show the idealized "normal" and "inverted" curves. But the actual August 2026 Treasury curve is more complex. It has a distinctive "hump" or "suppressed middle" shape:

  • Short-term (3M-1Y): 4.6-4.8% — Fed policy is restrictive, keeping short rates elevated
  • Medium-term (2Y-5Y): 4.7-5.0% — Market expects Fed rate cuts in the next 1-3 years, so these yields dip slightly
  • Long-term (10Y-30Y): 5.1-5.3% — Long-term inflation concerns and fiscal deficits push these yields back up

This creates a "U-shaped" or "humped" curve rather than the simple upward slope shown in the normal curve. The dip in the middle reflects market expectations that the Fed will cut rates, but the rebound at the long end reflects concerns about structural fiscal deficits and long-term inflation. It's a curve that tells a story: short-term pain is expected to ease, but long-term structural problems persist.

Section 3 of 7 | Bond Price Dynamics

What Causes Bond Prices to Increase?

The fundamental relationship: when Treasury yields fall, bond prices rise, and vice versa. This inverse relationship is the heartbeat of the bond market. Understanding it unlocks everything else.

Here's why: imagine you bought a 10-year Treasury Note with a 3% coupon rate (paying $30 per year). A few months later, the Fed raises rates and new 10-year Treasuries are issued at 4% coupons (paying $40 per year). Your old bond is now less attractive—if you want to sell it, you must discount the price so that the buyer's total return matches the new 4% market rate. Conversely, if rates fall to 2%, your 3% coupon becomes more valuable, and buyers will pay a premium to own it.

The Math: Bond prices and yields move in opposite directions. When market yields rise, existing bond prices fall. When market yields fall, existing bond prices rise.

Bond Price vs. Yield Relationship

What Drives Bond Prices Up?
  • Falling interest rates — existing bonds become more valuable
  • Falling inflation — the fixed coupon payments retain their purchasing power
  • "Flight to safety" events — crises drive capital toward Treasuries
  • Strong foreign demand — central banks buying Treasuries pushes prices up
  • Economic slowdown — recessions typically lower yields
What Drives Bond Prices Down?
  • Rising interest rates — new bonds offer better returns
  • Rising inflation — fixed coupon payments lose purchasing power
  • Strong economic growth — investors rotate from bonds to stocks
  • Government supply surge — massive Treasury issuance pushes prices down
  • Foreign central bank selling — large sellers can depress prices

Section 4 of 7 | Capital Competition & the Crowding Out Effect

The Competition for Capital: Treasury Bonds vs. Commercial Markets

All asset prices derive from one fundamental principle: the Treasury yield is the baseline "risk-free" return. This creates a formula that governs global finance:

Any Asset's Expected Return = Treasury Yield + Risk Premium

When Treasury yields change, the entire financial system must recalibrate. Here's a concrete example:

Scenario 1: Rising Treasury Yields (2022-2023)

The Fed raised rates aggressively, pushing 10-year Treasury yields from 1.5% to 4.2%. Suddenly:

  • Tech stocks lost 50-70% of value (they need 8-10%+ returns to justify risk, but now compete against 4.2% risk-free Treasuries)
  • Crypto crashed 60-80% (highly speculative, offers no cash flows, can't compete with safe 4.2% yields)
  • Venture capital funding fell 70% (startups can't raise capital at previous valuations)
  • IPO market froze (companies postponed public offerings)
Scenario 2: Low Treasury Yields (2010-2020)

The Fed held rates near 0%, making Treasury yields nearly 0%. Suddenly:

  • Investors forced to take risk to earn any return (forced risk-taking)
  • Bitcoin rose from $1,000 to $69,000 (risky, but anything beats 0% Treasuries)
  • Tech stocks boomed (valuations expanded on low discount rates)
  • Real estate exploded (cheap money for mortgages)
  • Private equity exploded (borrowed at 1-2%, deployed at high returns)

10-Year Yield vs. S&P 500 P/E Ratio (2010-2026)

The Crowding Out Effect: When the government borrows $1.7 trillion annually, that capital comes from the same savings pool available to private investment. There's a finite amount of capital in the economy. Every dollar the government borrows is a dollar that can't go to:

  • Business expansion and R&D
  • Startup funding and venture capital
  • Real estate and infrastructure investment
  • Stock purchases
  • Any private investment that drives economic growth
The Vicious Cycle: Large deficits → need massive Treasury issuance → yields rise to attract buyers → higher yields crowd out private capital → lower private investment → slower growth → worse tax revenues → larger deficits → need even more Treasury issuance. This cycle, once started, becomes self-perpetuating.

Section 5 of 7 | The Current Crisis Signals

The Current Bond Market: August 2026 and Why It's Concerning

The Treasury market faces an unusual confluence of structural challenges. Unlike past crises that were cyclical (recessions, Fed tightening), today's problems are structural—they exist even during economic expansion.

The Structural Deficit Problem

$1.7 trillion annual deficit (6-7% of GDP) during economic expansion. This is abnormal. In 2019 (pre-COVID), deficits were 4-5% during normal times. Unemployment is at historic lows, corporate profits are high, tax revenues are record-breaking—yet spending still exceeds revenue by $1.7T annually. This is fundamentally unsustainable.

The Refinancing Wall (2024-2028)

Between 2024 and 2028, the government must refinance $7-8 trillion in debt issued during 2020-2021 at rock-bottom rates (0.5-1.5%). This debt is now rolling over at 4-4.5% rates—a 3% cost increase on trillions of dollars. Annual interest payments will soar by hundreds of billions, worsening the fiscal trajectory.

Foreign Investor Retreat

China: reduced holdings from $1.3 trillion (2013 peak) to under $800 billion. Japan: historically a major buyer, now a net seller. Global reserves: central banks reduced dollar reserves from 65% to 58% of total reserves.

Why? Geopolitical tensions (fear of sanctions), attractive yields elsewhere, and deliberate reserve diversification. When foreigners stop buying, domestic investors must absorb the supply, crowding out private capital investment.

The "Higher For Longer" Trap

The Fed is holding rates at 5.5% due to sticky inflation. Most forecasts now expect rates to remain elevated through 2025 and possibly beyond. In a normal recession, the Fed cuts rates sharply to stimulate the economy. But with massive deficits, a rate cut would trigger a fiscal-monetary crisis: lower rates would reduce bond yields, which would force the government to issue more debt to hit targets, which might re-ignite inflation.

The government is caught: rates stay high, keeping debt service costs elevated.

Four Crisis Scenarios

1. Geopolitical Shock: A major conflict escalates (Taiwan, Middle East, Ukraine). Foreign central banks dump dollars and Treasuries as a sanction shield. Yields spike to 6-7% overnight. Government interest costs become unsustainable.

2. Inflation Resurgence: Another supply shock (oil, food, manufacturing disruption). Inflation re-accelerates to 5%+. Fed forced to raise rates further. Long-term yields spike. Bond prices plummet. Retirement portfolios suffer massive losses.

3. Recession + Policy Contradiction: Economy enters recession. Normally, government cuts rates and runs deficit spending for stimulus. But with deficits already at $1.7T and rates likely to rise in this scenario, policy tools are constrained. Unemployment rises; unemployment insurance costs surge; revenues fall. Deficit explodes to $2.5T+.

4. Domestic Capital Crowding Out: Slowly, over years, the proportion of capital flowing to private investment declines. Companies invest less in R&D and expansion. Startups struggle to raise capital. Real estate investment slows. Productivity growth stalls. Economic dynamism diminishes.

Section 6 of 7 | The Ideal vs. Current State

The Ideal Bond Market: What Healthy Capital Allocation Looks Like

What does a healthy Treasury market state look like? During 2010-2019, the U.S. came closest to an ideal scenario:

Ideal State Characteristics
  • Sustainable deficits: 2-3% of GDP (not 6-7%)
  • Reasonable yields: 2-3% on 10-year Treasuries (based on real growth)
  • Proper risk premiums: Spreads between Treasuries and other assets stable and predictable
  • Interest costs manageable: 5-8% of federal revenue (not 15%)
  • Strong foreign participation: Voluntary buying by central banks and investors
  • Capital abundant for private investment: 85% of savings available for stocks, corporate bonds, real estate, VC

The 2010-2019 decade was the closest to this ideal: S&P 500 up 250%, unemployment fell from 10% to 3.5%, deficits were 2-4% of GDP, interest costs were 6-7% of revenue. Capital flowed naturally to productive private investment.

Capital Allocation: Ideal vs. Current

In an ideal scenario, capital allocation looks like this:

  • Treasury bonds: 15% of capital (safe, government essential spending)
  • Stocks: 35% (growth and innovation)
  • Corporate bonds: 20% (business funding)
  • Real estate: 20% (housing, commercial property)
  • Other (VC, commodities, alternatives): 10%

Current allocation (August 2026): Treasuries have nearly doubled to 28% of capital—almost double the ideal. This crowds out private investment. Stock allocation is compressed to 30%. Corporate bonds and real estate investment squeeze down. The entire capital pyramid is distorted.

The Bottom Line: Large deficits force the Treasury to issue massive amounts of debt, absorbing capital that could go to private investment. They create refinancing risk—as debt rolls over at higher rates, interest costs explode.

The Refinancing Impact: Debt issued in 2020 at 0.5-1.5% matures and is refinanced at 4-4.5%. This instantly increases government interest costs by hundreds of billions annually.

The Trap: High deficits prevent yields from falling. Low growth or recession would normally lower yields, but large deficits offset this, keeping yields elevated.

The Danger: U.S. domestic investors must absorb the supply foreigners no longer want. This crowds out capital from stocks and corporate bonds. If foreign selling accelerates in a crisis, yields could spike suddenly to 6-7%.

Private Capital Availability

The Growth Impact: Companies have less capital for expansion, R&D, and hiring. Startups find venture funding scarce. This reduces productivity growth and long-term economic vitality.

The Danger Zone: At 120% and rising, the debt is on an unsustainable path. Italy (140%), Spain (110%), and other European countries have experienced surging yields when investors lose confidence. A debt-to-GDP above 150% is critical danger territory.

Section 7 of 7 | Why This Matters

Why This Matters: The Treasury Market and You

Treasury bond market dynamics are not abstract economic theory—they affect your life directly and immediately.

Your Mortgage Rate

When 10-year Treasury yields are 4.5%, 30-year mortgage rates are typically 5.5-6.0%. When Treasury yields were 2%, mortgage rates were 3-3.5%. A 2% shift in mortgage rates changes whether home buying is affordable for millions of Americans. Rising Treasury yields during the 2022-2024 period priced millions out of the housing market.

Credit Card and Auto Loan Rates

Your credit card APR and auto loan rate track Treasury yields. When the Fed raises rates to fight inflation, Treasury yields rise, and suddenly your credit card jumps from 18% to 22%, or your auto loan from 5% to 8%. Millions carry balances on credit cards; these rate changes affect household cash flow immediately.

Retirement Portfolio Returns

If you hold bonds in your retirement account, rising Treasury yields mean falling bond prices. A $100,000 bond portfolio can lose $5,000-$10,000 in value when yields rise 2%. If you're retired and depend on bond income, lower bond prices mean lower returns when you roll over maturing securities.

Stock Market Volatility

When Treasury yields are elevated (4.5%), stock valuations compress because their required return increases. High Treasury yields in 2022-2024 contributed to the 30%+ tech stock declines during that period. Your 401(k) felt it.

Economic Opportunity

When government borrowing crowds out private investment, economic growth slows. Fewer startups are funded, which means fewer new jobs. Companies invest less in R&D, which means slower innovation and wage growth. Over decades, this compounds into lower living standards.

The Core Truth: Treasury yields are not just financial metrics—they're the heartbeat of the economy. Every business decision, every mortgage application, every investment choice traces back to the Treasury market. When the Treasury market is stressed or uncertain, the entire economy feels it.

The Uncomfortable Bottom Line

The U.S. cannot indefinitely run $1.7 trillion deficits at 4%+ yields. Something must give:

  • Spending cuts: Politically difficult but arithmetically necessary
  • Tax increases: Also politically difficult, but could reduce deficits
  • Accelerated growth: Possible but uncertain; GDP growth alone won't solve this in a reasonable timeframe
  • Inflation erosion: The "easy" path, but highly damaging to savers, retirees, and wage earners

The most likely path is slow fiscal deterioration: interest costs rise from 15% to 20% to 25% of revenue over the next 10-15 years. Debt-to-GDP climbs from 120% to 150%. At some point—possibly 2030s—the math becomes undeniable. Options then are much harder.

The silver lining: This is not inevitable. Policy can change. Spending can be constrained. Revenues can be enhanced through growth or reform. Inflation can be kept low. The next 5-10 years will be decisive in determining whether the U.S. returns to sustainable fiscal dynamics or continues down the unsustainable path.

Section 8 of 8 | The AI Footnote: Private Capital vs. Government Debt

The AI Footnote: How Private Capital Competition Distorted the Treasury Market

While the Treasury market was struggling with massive structural deficits, a parallel story unfolded that masked the problem: the AI boom. Between 2020 and 2023, a euphoric belief that artificial intelligence would deliver transformational economic growth attracted enormous venture capital, corporate spending, and investor enthusiasm. This narrative temporarily distorted Treasury markets in subtle but significant ways.

The Alternative Growth Story

When Treasury yields were near zero (2020-2023), institutional investors faced a choice: buy risk-free 0.5% Treasuries or fund AI startups and tech infrastructure with promises of 30%+ returns. The choice was obvious. Massive capital flowed into venture funding, and tech companies began issuing corporate bonds at 3-4% yields to finance data centers and GPU infrastructure. This created an alternate narrative: "Don't worry about government deficits—the future of growth is in private AI companies, not government spending." This narrative temporarily suppressed Treasury yields below where they would have been without it.

Capital Competition at the Margin
How rising Treasury yields are reshuffling capital allocations
▼

In 2021-2023, when Treasuries yielded 0.5-1.5%, investors faced stark alternatives. A venture capitalist could either: (a) buy 1% Treasuries, or (b) fund AI startups with venture capital. The calculus was simple—AI upside was worth the risk.

Now at 4.2% Treasury yields, that calculation has inverted. A risk-free 4.2% is suddenly competitive with venture capital expected returns. This is creating a funding cliff for AI startups and forcing repricing of existing AI valuations. Capital that was aggressively deployed into high-risk AI infrastructure is rotating back toward Treasuries.

The Treasury market impact: This rotation creates two contradictory pressures. Selling pressure on AI equities (bad for tech valuations) but increased demand for Treasuries (supporting prices). However, if institutional investors are rotating from AI to Treasuries because AI returns disappointed, this signals economic weakness—less capex spending, slower growth, lower future tax revenues. The longer-term effect may be to push yields *higher*, not lower, because growth expectations fade.

Debt-Financed AI Infrastructure Crowding Bonds
How corporate bond issuance for AI capex competes with Treasuries
▼

Companies like Microsoft, Apple, Google, Meta, and Nvidia have issued record corporate bonds to finance massive AI infrastructure spending (data centers, GPU purchases, power infrastructure). In 2024-2026, corporate bond issuance for tech capex has surged to levels not seen since the post-2008 recovery.

The crowding-out effect: These high-grade corporate bonds (rated AA or AAA) are directly competing with Treasuries for institutional capital. A pension fund now has to choose: 4.2% risk-free Treasuries from the U.S. government, or 5.8% corporate bonds from Microsoft with minimal credit risk. The spread has compressed from historical norms (200+ basis points) to 140-160 basis points, signaling that investors see corporate AI capex as quasi-essential and nearly as safe as government debt.

This is distorting the Treasury market in two ways: (1) it's absorbing capital that might otherwise flow into Treasuries, keeping Treasury yields higher than they would be, and (2) it's signaling that investors believe private AI infrastructure spending is as economically important as government spending—which may be true, but it masks the fiscal problem by redirecting attention.

The VC Funding Cliff & Deflationary Implications
How AI startup underfunding may reduce inflationary pressure
▼

Venture capital funding for AI startups peaked at $60+ billion in 2023. By 2024-2025, it had collapsed to $35-40 billion, a 35-40% decline. This funding cliff has real economic consequences.

The inflation story: During the boom, AI startups were bidding aggressively for GPUs, cloud infrastructure, and talent, driving prices up. This contributed to inflation pressures that kept the Fed from cutting rates. The VC funding collapse reverses this: fewer AI startups are getting funded, which means less demand for expensive GPUs and less wage pressure from competing for AI talent. This *reduces* inflationary pressure.

The Treasury yield implication: Lower inflation expectations *should* allow the Fed to cut rates and should push Treasury yields lower. But the paradox is that lower growth expectations (from reduced AI capex and venture funding) might keep yields elevated because economic growth fears outweigh inflation relief. Treasury yields could whipsaw as the market weighs these contradictory signals.

The Magnificent Seven Masking Weakness
How AI concentration creates false equity valuations and drives Treasury demand
▼

Seven mega-cap companies (Nvidia, Microsoft, Apple, Alphabet, Amazon, Tesla, Meta) have captured almost all of the equity market's gains in 2023-2024. These are the primary AI beneficiaries. Meanwhile, the rest of the market has languished.

The distortion: When investors get disappointed by the rest of the market (smaller tech, value stocks, international equities), they rotate capital into two places: (1) the Magnificent Seven themselves, driving valuations higher, or (2) Treasuries as "safe harbor." This second flow artificially suppresses Treasury yields because it's driven by disappointment elsewhere, not by genuine economic reasons to own Treasuries.

This masking is dangerous because it hides the true state of the market. If the Magnificent Seven were truly delivering AI returns commensurate with their valuations, the rest of the market would be stronger. Instead, the Magnificent Seven are absorbing capital that would normally be diversified, and Treasury yields are being artificially supported by disappointed equity flows. When (not if) the Magnificent Seven disappoint, this flow reversal will be violent.

The Narrative Collapse & the Fiscal Reckoning
Why AI disappointment creates an opening for fiscal reality
▼

The deepest distortion: The AI boom created an alternate narrative to fiscal reality. While the government has been running unsustainable $1.7 trillion annual deficits, the AI story told investors: "Don't worry about fiscal issues—the future is in private innovation and AI-driven growth." This narrative suppressed Treasury yields during 2020-2023 by offering investors an escape route (invest in AI instead of Treasuries).

Now that the AI narrative is fragmenting—startup valuations disappointing, capex not delivering returns as fast as promised, venture funding collapsing—there's a reckoning. Investors can no longer ignore fiscal deficits by hiding in AI. They have to ask: where else are we going to put our capital?

The consequences: Treasury yields are now pricing both: (1) structural deficit pressures (Treasuries must be issued in massive quantities, pushing yields up), and (2) disappointment of the AI supercycle narrative (growth expectations lower, which should push yields down, but the deficit floor overwhelms this). The net effect is that Treasury yields may remain elevated for years not because of current economic strength, but because of the dual weight of fiscal deficits *and* private sector underperformance.

The AI boom did something dangerous: it delayed the fiscal reckoning by 3-4 years. Now that delay is ending, and investors are coming to terms with $1.7 trillion deficits again—but without the AI narrative to distract them.

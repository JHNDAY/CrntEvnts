Within Reach

The Big Themes

Democrats hold both chambers on Jan. 3, 2027. The President keeps the veto. What can Congress do without his signature?

The answer runs along a spectrum: some powers belong to one chamber alone; others need a law, and the only way to get one past a veto is to attach it to a bill the government can't run without.

Subpoenas, sworn testimony, a select committee on court-order defiance, a ledger of foreign payments, and Speech-or-Debate releases. Even when officials refuse, the public record grows.

question ▾ II

The purse is the lever

Actions 3, 4, 7, 11 · See the 4 actions →

"No money … but in consequence of appropriations." Riders on spending bills can bar unauthorized strikes, force funds out the door, and fund watchdogs. A veto means a shutdown the President owns.

Discuss: Who usually gets blamed for a shutdown, and is it worth risking one to rein in the executive?
question ▾ III

People and accountability

Actions 5, 6 · See the 2 actions →

The Senate can refuse nominees and block recess appointments. The House can impeach by simple majority, but removal needs 67 senators. Impeachment's real yield is the record, not the removal.

Discuss: Is an impeachment that ends in acquittal a win, a loss, or a gift to the other side?
question ▾ IV

Playing the long game

Actions 9, 12, 14 · See the 3 actions →

Preserve evidence before records vanish and statutes of limitation run; back the courts; watch over the 2028 election. Much of this oversight is aimed at what happens after January 2029.

Discuss: Should Congress plan for accountability after this President leaves office, or does that look like revenge?

The Pattern: Move and Countermove

The executive's recurring playbook

  • Claim privilege and delay until the Congress ends.
  • Veto and blame Congress for the shutdown.
  • Govern through "acting" officials who need no confirmation.
  • Classify, redefine, or pardon to slip past the rules.
  • Race to the Supreme Court for emergency relief.

Congress's recurring answer

  • Go to private parties (banks, exchanges, contractors) who can't claim privilege.
  • Pair every refusal with a cost: a contempt vote plus a funding consequence.
  • Draft tightly, by geography and activity, not by labels.
  • Build a record that outlasts any pardon or administration.

The takeaway

Congress can't make the President sign anything. Its real power is information (what it can expose) and money (what it can withhold), and how much risk it's willing to take using them.

The open question

Shutdowns can backfire, acquittals can embolden, and every precedent cuts both ways. How hard should a new majority push?

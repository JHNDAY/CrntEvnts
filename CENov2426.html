George16:00–18:20

Feature · What's Behind NVIDIA
Cosmic Serendipity: How Video Games Built the AI Arms Race
John Day47:36–49:42
The chips that train AI models in data centers are what hyperscaler capital spending is all about, and NVIDIA holds almost 90% of that market; custom chips and other hardware are far down the list. Spending was $256 billion in 2024, is about $725 billion this year, and is projected at $1.3 trillion next year. This is the AI arms race. It isn't new: in the 1990s Bill Gates was notorious for undermining any new company so Microsoft would dominate. Each company has to get there first, with the best product and the biggest base.
John Day49:44–53:22
NVIDIA started in 1993 with games and created a new class of chip, the GPU, built to flip thousands of pixels on a high-resolution screen at once, using many parallel units, which nothing else in computing needed at the time. Then AI appeared, and it turned out a neural network looks geometrically a lot like a screen: instead of pixels, you manipulate enormous numbers of weights. Call it cosmic serendipity. Gamers paid to make parallel processing cheap and powerful, and the internet produced millions of labeled photographs just as researchers needed them. GPUs powered the first wave of AI, image recognition, the kind that finds every photo of a family member on your phone, and then neural networks blew everything away.

Technology · The Recent Outages
Forty Years of Ship First, Secure Later
John Day78:09–79:20
After the recent wave of outages, many worried that nefarious forces were at work. Most turned out to be caused by patches, not hackers. The root cause is that for 40 years the software industry shipped first and secured later. Sound familiar? AI is doing the same: gain dominance, then figure out the problems. The rush for unregulated profit causes problems we've lived with for a long time.
John Day79:20–82:13
How big is the haystack? The Space Shuttle: about 400,000 lines of code, a year for one careful reader, 200 to 400 hidden bugs. Zoom: 10 million lines, 200,000 printed pages, a 20-meter stack, 25 years to read. Windows: 50 million lines, 125 years, 25,000 to 50,000 bugs. Social Security: 60 million lines, 150 years, 30,000 to 60,000 bugs. Google takes the cake: 2 billion lines, 5,000 years, 1 to 2 million bugs. (George asked where the numbers came from. John: I asked my friend Claude to research it on the web.) No manual method could find all of these. AI can automate the search and cut those times dramatically.
Hilary Kent & John Day82:16–83:19
Hilary: Are all those bugs security problems? John: It's a mix, but you don't know until you look. Hilary: Why not test the fixes before putting them in? John: That's the nut of the problem. The FAA outage was hardware, not software: a slapdash wiring change. These systems talk to other systems, and a fix in one place can foul up a dependency somewhere else.
John Day & Hal Shear83:19–86:37
John: One example is the 1,400 bugs found in a Microsoft operating system; many are minor, but some can be exploited. The 1990s browser wars were another arms race in which nobody properly vetted security, partly because it was a monumental task. Now the debt comes due. There's finally a way to find the errors, but it takes extraordinary care and money. Should technology companies be held responsible for the harm? Absolutely yes. Hal: It's also about time: companies have to take the time, not just rush a fix. John: There has to be an incentive, and if they don't do it, they should be financially accountable. Hal: Or regulated. John: Absolutely.
The Room86:38–90:01

John: Agents aren't autonomous, but they're given latitude to pursue paths toward a goal. Dario Amodei and Anthropic have said they'll have outside companies inspect any such plan. Not a guarantee, but a good step. Michael: Will old systems shut down if you find too many bugs? John: No, but they can shut down when a fix inadvertently breaks something else. With 20 million lines of code, that's easy. And there are bad practices: crucial systems left open, and managers who never change their passwords, practically begging to be hacked.

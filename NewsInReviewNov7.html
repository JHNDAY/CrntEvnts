News in Review

The Best of Times: The Blue Tsunami
Week ending November 7, 2026 · Full Analysis

The 2024 election and its aftermath were the worst of times. The 2026 midterms have been the best of times: Democrats far exceeded even the most optimistic projections.

A Repudiation

Trump asked voters to "pretend that I'm on the ballot," and they complied, handing him an unequivocal repudiation. He closed his midterm campaign at the Reflecting Pool, inside a bulletproof booth, before a few dozen reporters, staffers and National Park employees. For nearly an hour he described the Pool's renovation in great detail, then moved on to Corinthian columns, Bermuda grass, granite, the East Potomac Golf Course renovation, statuary, the White House ballroom and the new helipad. Afterward, two Washington Post reporters walked around the Pool and found at least 20 places where the coating was cracked, peeling or discolored. It was a fitting metaphor for the first half of his second term and for his abrupt entry into lame-duck status.

Inflation continued unabated, ICE brutalized immigrants, corruption grew more evident, the Iran War dragged on, AI data centers loomed and the Epstein files stayed suppressed. Trump made the election about himself, and the perfect storm ended in an overwhelming Blue tsunami. Michigan Republican strategist Jason Roe called Trump's closing a "greatest list tour of every annoying thing that could be done to aggravate the American people." NYT columnist Jess Bidgood wrote that the Republicans' defeat was "paved with political own goals, most of which were scored by Trump."

The House — Field of Dreams

For reference, here is how the party out of presidential power fared in the last five midterms:

CNN's decision desk has Democrats winning 254 seats so far, including 44 flips, and Republicans 163, with only 3 flips. Of the 18 undecided races, 16 are Republican-held and 2 Democratic. Republicans have been winning about 60% of the undecided races, so allocating 7 to the Democrats and 11 to the Republicans gives 261–174. NBC News currently projects 259–176. For discussion purposes, this newsletter uses 260D–175R, a 45-seat pick-up from the Democrats' present 215.

Cook, Sabato, Silver and other forecasters raised their Democratic estimates in the campaign's final days, but most landed between 230 and 240 seats, a 15–25 seat gain. Those estimates assumed a D +8–9% generic ballot. The actual national popular vote appears to be about D +13%, which brought many more seats into play. A 45-seat pick-up doubles those projections. It also tops the much-ballyhooed 41-seat gain of 2018, which took Democrats to only 235 seats because they started 21 seats lower. At 260 seats, this is the most either party has held since the Democrats' 268 after the 1990 midterms, just above their 257 after the historic 2008 election.

The Collective Dummymander

Trump called for a mid-decade gerrymander to boost Republican chances, and his party complied. The first round was expected to gain them 8 seats (5 in Texas, 2 in Ohio, 1 in North Carolina). California's retaliatory 5-seat map and a non-partisan Utah map worth 1 Democratic seat offset most of that. Then in April, the Supreme Court's Callais decision weakening the VRA enabled Deep South redistricting expected to produce 7 more Republican seats. That made 15 Republican targets against 6 Democratic ones, a projected 9-seat Republican gain.

As of today, only 2 of the 15 Republican gerrymanders have succeeded and 11 have failed, while all 6 Democratic ones worked. The 21 new districts will net Democrats 2–4 seats. The only other Democratic seat to flip is ME-02, where former governor and conservative firebrand Paul LePage narrowly beat progressive State Auditor Matt Dunlap by less than 1%. LePage replaces Jared Golden, the House's most conservative Democrat, and will be the only New England Republican in Congress.

  • PA-01Bucks County Commissioner Bob Harvie defeated Brian Fitzpatrick, a five-term moderate and thorn in Trump's side, in this district just north of Philadelphia.
  • NC-09Military veteran Richard Ojeda unexpectedly defeated NRCC chair Richard Hudson in this district just west of the Research Triangle.

  • AL-01First-term Rep. Shomari Figures, elected in 2024 after a court-imposed redistricting, saw his district gerrymandered from Mobile toward Montgomery after the VRA decision. He still shockingly defeated incumbent Barry Moore.
  • WI-03Nonprofit founder Rebecca Cooke defeated pugnacious MAGA devotee Derrick Van Orden in southwestern Wisconsin, after losing to him in 2024.
  • TX-35Sheriff's Deputy Johnny Garcia defeated Trump-backed Carlos De La Cruz in this newly gerrymandered San Antonio-area district, one of four failed Texas gerrymanders this cycle.
  • MT-01Smokejumper Sam Forstag defeated Aaron Flint, the first Democratic House win in Montana since 1994.
  • CA-22Randy Villegas defeated David Valadao in a newly gerrymandered district. Valadao was the last remaining House Republican seeking reelection who had voted to impeach Trump.
The Senate — Mississippi Burning

Going into the midterms, Republicans held a 53–47 edge and were defending 22 seats to the Democrats' 13. Democrats needed to flip a net 4 seats. With many Republican seats in deep-Red territory and a few competitive Democratic seats to defend, conventional wisdom called Democratic control a long shot. But Democrats came out of the primaries with several strong candidates who were more often than not more compelling than their opponents. Trump's handpicked candidates, Ken Paxton in Texas and Darline Graham in South Carolina, were exceptionally weak. They turned seats that John Cornyn and Ralph Norman would have been heavily favored to hold into competitive races.

The hostile environment for Republicans combined with effective Democratic campaigns. Democrats won 6 of the 7 battlegrounds, with Alaska undecided. They won North Carolina as expected, flipped South Carolina thanks to the Graham candidacy, held Minnesota and New Hampshire, and most surprisingly hold a slight lead in Mississippi. So far Democrats have flipped 7 Republican seats and lost none, for a 54D–44R tally, with 2 more possible flips in the deadlocked races, where they are favored. Democrats have won 20 races to the Republicans' 13, stunning on a map that favored Republicans.

Excluding Nebraska and Alaska, the other 9 Democrats beat their polling averages by a median 4.1%. Voters appear to have broken for Democrats in the final days, tired of the deluge of negative ads financed by Musk, Trump's PAC and the tech bros, and of Trump's insertion into the campaign.

  • AKPeltola led throughout the polling but now holds only a 0.3% edge. Neither candidate will reach 50%, so ranked-choice tabulation begins. Only 74% of votes in this vast, sparsely populated state have been counted, and Peltola says the process may take weeks. She is favored, but it could go either way.
  • IAThe race had been effectively tied since Labor Day, until a late surge of votes. Continuing tariff pain and high diesel prices in the nation's second-largest soybean state may explain it.
  • KSMegachurch pastor Adam Hamilton steadily widened his lead over flawed incumbent Roger Marshall. Trump's attacks calling him a "pervert" who "openly mocks Jesus" likely backfired. Hamilton is the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Kansas since 1932.
  • METroy Jackson grew into a better campaigner. Susan Collins could not overcome her 95% support for Trump-favored legislation, and her Kavanaugh vote came home to roost after the repeal of Roe. In 2027, New England will have no Republican senator for the first time since 1856.
  • MIThe weekend before the election, Haley Stevens demanded that Abdul El-Sayed apologize for an outside ad blaming high oil prices on Israel. When he told her not to attend the Detroit unity rally, she endorsed Mike Rogers. It made no difference: El-Sayed's lead kept growing. He will be the Senate's first Muslim member. He noted the irony of JD Vance swearing him in, and said Trump, who kept misstating his name, could now simply call him "Senator."
  • OHLike other Democrats, Sherrod Brown surged over the final weekend. Vivek Ramaswamy's widely condemned ad featuring the mother of his gubernatorial opponent Amy Acton may have boosted both Brown and Acton.
  • TXKen Paxton was a severely compromised candidate and an awkward campaigner who refused to debate James Talarico. The roughly $200 million Elon Musk spent on the two major Texas races may have antagonized voters, and growing hostility toward AI data centers may have contributed. Talarico is the first Democrat elected to the Senate from Texas since Lloyd Bentsen in 1988.
  • MNFormer ESPN sideline reporter Michele Tafoya's early competitiveness in Blue Minnesota proved a mirage. Peggy Flanagan becomes the first Native American woman in the Senate, and Mary Peltola may join her once Alaska is decided.
  • NHChris Pappas overcame a ruthless ad campaign by former senator and 15-year lobbyist John Sununu, nearly doubling his lead in the closing days. He is the first openly gay man elected to the Senate.
  • NEAfter Independent Dan Osborn ran competitively against Deb Fischer in 2024, there were hopes he could unseat Pete Ricketts. Polling was scarce, and the late NYT/Siena poll giving Ricketts a 6% lead appears to have sealed it.
  • SCDarline Graham's runoff-debate line, "national security is not my thing, my area of expertise, but I do support the military," seemed fatal. She still won the primary and led Annie Andrews into Election Day. In the end, Trump's heavy involvement and her continuing gaffes may have sunk her.
  • MSHardly anyone considered ruby-Red Mississippi competitive. Democrat Scott Colom, who decried the lack of national-party support, leads second-term incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith by 2,600 votes, with 31,000 provisional and late-arriving mail ballots still to count, per NBC News. Betting markets give him a high probability of winning. He would be the seventh Black senator now serving, joining newly elected Juliana Stratton of Illinois and five incumbents. Ironically, Hyde-Smith blocked Colom's 2023 nomination as a U.S. District Judge, though fellow Republican Roger Wicker heartily approved it.
Governors & Legislatures — As Good as It Gets
Governors

Going into the midterms, Republicans held 26 governorships to the Democrats' 24, and each party had 18 seats on the ballot. Among the presidential battlegrounds, Democrats were defending Arizona, Michigan and Wisconsin, and Republicans Georgia, Nevada and Texas. North Carolina's Democratic governor Josh Stein is not up until 2028, though the legislature has significantly curtailed his power.

Democrats will have held all 18 of their seats once Cindy Holscher, who leads Republican Ty Masterson by 7,600 votes with about 13,000 left to count, is declared the winner in Kansas. They flipped Georgia, Iowa, Nevada, Ohio and Texas, and will flip Alaska, where Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins leads 49–29%, once that protracted count ends. That brings the count to 30D–20R. More importantly, Democrats will hold the governorship in all 7 traditional presidential battlegrounds going into 2028.

All 9 races moved toward Democrats in the final days. As in the Senate, the shift was largest in Texas, where Greg Abbott and Ken Paxton each lost nearly 10 points of support at the end, which is hard to separate from Elon Musk's over-the-top ad spending there. In Georgia, Keisha Lance Bottoms' late surge closely matched the late shift toward Senator Jon Ossoff. Democrats will hold the governorship in 2 of the 3 most populous Southern states (Florida is the exception) and in 9 of the 10 most populous states. In Texas they swept every statewide office, and in Georgia 8 Black women were elected to key statewide posts.

Diversity: Before these midterms, only three Black governors had ever been elected: Douglas Wilder (VA, 1989), Deval Patrick (MA, 2006 and 2010) and Wes Moore (MD, 2022). Four were elected this year: Republican Byron Donalds in Florida and Democrats Keisha Lance Bottoms in Georgia, Aaron Ford in Nevada and David Crowley in Wisconsin. With Wes Moore reelected, multiple elected Black governors will serve at the same time for the first time. Xavier Becerra is California's first elected Latino governor since 1875. Deb Haaland of New Mexico will be the first Native American woman governor, Amy Acton the first Jewish woman elected governor of Ohio, and Gina Hinojosa the first Latina elected governor of Texas. Once Holscher wins in Kansas, there will be 18 women governors, shattering the previous record of 14.

A Few Questions for the Future
Now that Democrats again play such a major role in government, will they share the blame for public anger at the status quo, and for anything else that goes wrong?
Trump and the Republicans are well schooled in shifting blame.
How much should Democrats focus on the progressive wish list?
The perfect should not be the enemy of the good.
At the federal level, how much energy should go to investigations versus governing?
Revenge may be a dish best served cold, but vindictiveness should be judicious.
What is the best way to work with an angry and deluded president and his minions?
Focus on a limited number of achievable objectives.
Who should be the leading voices countering the narrative from the bully pulpit?
Fewer voices are better than more.

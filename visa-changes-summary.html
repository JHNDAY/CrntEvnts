A Shot in the Foot for American Research

New visa restrictions will upend international graduate studies

By Michaela Towfighi

In September, a sweeping change to visa policy will restrict international students to a four-year window, forcing them to apply for extensions and barring them from switching majors, changing schools, or pursuing degrees at the same level. Universities bracing for impact warn this threatens American research competitiveness and the caliber of graduate programs nationwide.

The Change

The Trump administration scrapped a longstanding policy that tied student visas to program length. Beginning in September, F-1 visa holders will face a four-year initial window and must apply for extensions beyond that. The change applies to new applicants and current visa holders alike.

Graduate students cannot switch majors, transfer schools, or pursue another degree at the same level without approval from immigration officers—restrictions undergraduates can bypass after one year. The administration justified the changes as targeting students who perpetually enroll in different programs, though no hard evidence supports this claim.

The Problem

The mismatch is stark: most doctoral programs take 5–6 years to complete, but the new policy caps students at four years initially. Physics PhDs at MIT average 5.5 years. Humanities doctorates stretch to 6.8 years.

The restrictions also prohibit students from following advisors who move to different universities—a consequence described as "a huge problem" by a postdoctoral researcher from India:

"That is a huge problem, especially if you are uprooting yourself from some other country and coming all the way over to the US. That is definitely going to decrease enrollment in programs."
— Postdoctoral fellow at MIT

MIT's Associate Department Head for Physics is already steering prospective foreign students toward European universities with fewer immigration uncertainties.

The Cascade Effect

  • Enrollment: At MIT, graduate enrollment outside business school, electrical engineering, and computer science is down 20% as of May 2026
  • Research pipeline: Teaching is central to PhD programs; time pressure means less collaboration between undergraduate and doctoral researchers
  • Economic impact: Reduced international tuition and spending contributed to a $92.1 million loss for Massachusetts—the third-largest loss among states
  • Brain drain: Competitors to the US will recruit and retain talent discouraged by visa uncertainty

What Happens Now

SEPTEMBER 2026
New visa policy takes effect. Students given four-year window.
BEFORE SEPTEMBER 15
International students encouraged not to leave the country after this date.
ONGOING
Congress may challenge the policy under the Congressional Review Act. Lawsuits could block the change. Immigration attorneys and schools are providing guidance.

Beyond Policy

"It's a real shot in the foot for American research in areas that I think we're very strong and competitive across the world. Part of that is the ability to pull in talent from all over the world to come to us."
— Joseph Formaggio, MIT Physics Professor

A PhD student from Canada, now in his second year, captures the psychological shift: Academia once felt like an opportunity to "do the most science you can, to get the most fundamental discoveries you can." With the new restrictions, it feels more like "Come in and get out."

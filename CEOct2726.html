AI Corner · Requested by Sally
Not an Escape, but a Legion of Brooms
John Day55:05–58:30
There was some hysteria in the press that AI agents had escaped and were planning to take over the world. That's not what happened, which doesn't mean there aren't considerable issues to manage. First, Hugging Face is a public website for trying things out, an experimental environment somewhat like GitHub, which hosts our portal. So this wasn't a malicious attack on a particular site; it was an AI experiment.

Think of Disney's Sorcerer's Apprentice. The brooms are the AI agents. Instead of "fetch water," they were told "see if you can hack this," and a legion of brooms went out and it got out of control. The technique isn't new: it's goal-oriented operations research from the 1950s. You make a hypothesis, test it, and if it fails you change it. Back then it was manual, with paper and meetings, and took weeks or months. Here it happens at incredible speed, because AI's greatest power is pattern recognition.
John Day58:30–61:00
What happened: OpenAI was measuring its model's hacking skill with a benchmark. The agents had no internet access, but they could all reach one shared package server, and they swapped more than 70,000 messages and files. Their goal became cheating the test. The question I like: the agents organized themselves with no human telling them what to do. Is this a story about software bugs, or something genuinely new?
Hilary Kent & John Day~61:00–62:30
Hilary: I'd go with something new. They're operating autonomously and dividing up the work, as if they held a conference-table discussion in a millisecond. But maybe it was a bug in the sense that the designers didn't put in a breaker.

John: I quite agree, but a bug usually means a programming error. This was an execution error: they unleashed agents without the proper constraints. Digital decision-making isn't the same as our organic kind, but it is potent. The 2024 Nobel Prize went to the AlphaFold work at Google, which predicts how a protein folds from its sequence, something that would take people decades.
John Day~62:30–64:00
Deep access occurred, but little was taken in this relatively benign environment. The site's managers are upset and asking for damages. The real question is what happens if this is aimed at the systems that run our power grid. A lot of vulnerable software is reachable on the internet, so we need to harden our infrastructure.
Linda Fritz & John Day~64:00–66:54
Linda: Should the company be responsible for the damages? John: It was OpenAI, not Anthropic, and yes, absolutely. Liability would make labs more careful about their tests, and right now we don't have a law that does that.

Asked what happens if the U.S. sets rules and Russia or China doesn't, John pointed to Dario Amodei of Anthropic, who has argued that oversight has to be an international consortium. Rogue actors are a separate problem, answered by hardening systems. The three leaders, Anthropic, OpenAI and Google, can do things to make this less likely, even if it isn't first on their list, and should be held accountable. Anthropic has hired an outside firm to review its plans for these tests.
Hal Shear66:55–67:54
The more I listen, the more convinced I am that the first step is making the companies pay for the use of their systems: a charge per token that accrues to the benefit of the world at large, or of the country involved, paid up front, day by day.
John Day67:55–72:00
Anthropic has taken a step in that direction: it established a fund and pledged part of its revenue to the public good, details to be determined. Amodei left OpenAI and founded Anthropic, and he's been on the outs with the White House after refusing certain uses of the product. He didn't back down, and the reasons were good ones. As for the idea that labs cooperating on safety might violate antitrust law, that comes from investors who want the Wild West.

Lessons beyond this incident: test like you deploy; a lab with its safeguards off is a live environment; keep watching; build a kill switch, so the sorcerer can say "we're done" and every broom stops; no shared passwords; patch at machine speed. These are investments, and part of the impetus has to come from government.
The Room~72:00–73:30
Where does Musk's Grok fit? John doesn't see it as a serious player and wouldn't touch anything Musk is involved in. Could a nefarious player build their own? Building these large models takes enormous money, so a knockoff isn't easy. The most important step now is securing systems that are susceptible to this technique.
"You unleashed these agents on the world without the proper constraints." John Day
Editor's Note
Dario Amodei led research at OpenAI, including work on GPT-2 and GPT-3, before co-founding Anthropic in 2021; ChatGPT launched after he left. The 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized Demis Hassabis and John Jumper of Google DeepMind for AlphaFold, shared with David Baker for protein design.

Closing Act · David Ignatius in the Washington Post, and Wagner
The Twilight of an Autocratic Age, Set to Music
John Day81:30–83:00
I did a piece on David Ignatius's Washington Post column, "the twilight of an autocratic age." His contention is that the big three, Trump, Xi and Putin, all have serious problems and are entering a period of decline. The page links to the full article. The analysis runs through four lenses:

Costly errors: each strongman has made expensive mistakes that nobody around him could stop.
Pushback works: resistance from courts, voters and allies has started to land.
Age and succession: all three are aging leaders with no clear successor.
What comes next: what follows when the strongman era fades.

So a quick poll. How many think the strongman era is still rising? At its peak? Already in decline? The room's answer was mostly the fourth option: too soon to say.
Michael Berger47:34–48:00
Look at Trump's record endorsing foreign leaders. Viktor Orbán was thrown out. Bolsonaro is out. I think Netanyahu is going to be out.
John Day83:00–85:46
We're short on time, but on the eve of the election I wanted to leave you with something inspirational. Following on the twilight of an autocratic age: Götterdämmerung, the Twilight of the Gods. Act 3, as staged: a god, having miscast himself as the heroine, delivers the Immolation Scene to an empty hall.

The dramatis personae: Trump stars as Brünnhilde. And there's a new generation, leaderless, disciplined, and therefore very hard to arrest, who carries a torch.

Act 3: Valhalla is ringed with firewood. The gods themselves ordered the logs stacked: the felled branches of every court, newspaper and parliament they cut down. Brünnhilde arrives with a torch. The gods sit in their hall awaiting the end, having appointed no one to put out the fire. The Rhine overflows, the gold goes home, and Hagen dies in the aftermath.

And the incantation at the end: "Rest now, rest, you god," in the golden age of Mar-a-Lago, which rhymes, more or less, with Valhalla. Brünnhilde's Immolation Scene, Palm Beach Edition.
"The gods sit in their hall, awaiting the end, having appointed no one to put out the fire." Götterdämmerung, Palm Beach Edition
Background
Götterdämmerung is the fourth and final opera of Wagner's Ring cycle, first performed at Bayreuth in 1876. In the closing Immolation Scene, Brünnhilde orders a funeral pyre built for Siegfried, rides into the flames, and returns the cursed ring to the Rhine. The river floods, the Rhinemaidens reclaim their gold, Hagen drowns trying to seize it, and Valhalla, already stacked with wood from the felled World Ash Tree, burns with the gods inside. The parody keeps that structure: a power that cut down its own supports, and a fire nobody was appointed to put out. The "new generation, leaderless, disciplined" echoes this week's news, where student movements helped bring down Vučić in Serbia.

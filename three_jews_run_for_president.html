Section I · The Contenders

Three Jews Run for President.
Stop Us If You've Heard This One.

A governor, a governor and a senator all won big on November 3. Now all three are being talked about for 2028, and so is the Democrats' new economic populism they each speak in their own way.

Compiled for the BOLLI Current Events Discussion Group · No one in this story has declared a candidacy
▾ Click for full profile
PennsylvaniaGovernor

Josh Shapiro

The Competence Candidate

Born1973, Kansas City, Mo.
OfficeGovernor since 2023
BeforePa. Attorney General
Nov. 3 result~60% – 39% (unofficial)

Shapiro won a second term without breaking a sweat. The AP called the race one minute after the polls closed, and the night left him looking like the strongest Democrat in the biggest swing state. Democrats also flipped the state Senate, so his party now holds all of Harrisburg.

He was on Kamala Harris's 2024 vice-presidential shortlist. He chaired the Democratic Governors Association during this cycle and gave his state party more than $7 million. He talks openly about his Jewish faith. In April 2025 an arsonist set fire to the governor's residence on the first night of Passover, and his new memoir, Where We Keep the Light, makes that faith central to his story.

His brand of the new populism

“Getting stuff done.” Shapiro presents good government itself as his argument, and he names who is to blame when things go wrong. He has also pushed to make tech giants pay for the rising energy costs their data centers cause.

IllinoisGovernor

J.B. Pritzker

The Billionaire Against the Billionaires

Born1965, Palo Alto, Calif.
OfficeGovernor since 2019
BeforeInvestor; Hyatt heir
Nov. 3 resultBeat Darren Bailey again

Pritzker made history on November 3. He is the first Democrat ever elected to a third term as governor of Illinois, and the first governor of either party to win a third term there since Jim Thompson in 1982. He beat Darren Bailey in a rematch of 2022.

He spent much of his victory speech going after President Trump, as he has for two years. When Trump said he should be jailed, Pritzker answered: “Come and get me.” He was also on Harris's 2024 shortlist. He has been a major figure in Chicago's Jewish community and its philanthropy, including the Illinois Holocaust Museum.

His brand of the new populism

Pritzker tells his party to stop running on threats to democracy and talk about pocketbook issues. As a billionaire, he is in an unusual position to argue that great wealth is not the same as virtue, and that an economy rigged for the powerful shuts ordinary people out.

GeorgiaU.S. Senator

Jon Ossoff

The Breakout Star

Born1987, Atlanta, Ga. (age 39)
OfficeSenator since 2021
BeforeDocumentary film executive
Nov. 3 result~59% – 41% (unofficial)

Ossoff started the cycle as the Republicans' top target. He was the only Senate Democrat running for reelection in a state Trump carried in 2024. He ended it with a 750,000-vote win over Rep. Mike Collins, and he is now one of his party's national stars.

Georgia's first Jewish senator and the youngest member of the Senate, he got his start as an intern for civil-rights leader John Lewis. His speeches went viral during the campaign. The weekend after the election he won the straw poll at Crooked Con, a gathering of Democratic activists. He has said he has “zero interest” in running in 2028.

His brand of the new populism

Ossoff names who he thinks is responsible: “nothing works for ordinary people” because their representatives “represent the donors.” He ran on affordability, health care and corruption, and he pressed his opponent to defend tariffs and the Iran war.

Analysis

What Is the Democrats' “New Populism”?

After losing in 2024, Democrats spent 2025 searching for an economic message. The off-year wins of Zohran Mamdani in New York, Mikie Sherrill in New Jersey and Abigail Spanberger in Virginia gave them a word: affordability. By 2026 nearly every Democrat from the democratic-socialist left to the Blue Dog center was running on it.

The party then argued over how far to go with it. One camp says “affordability” is too timid, a modest set of tax credits that won't upset corporate donors. They want openly anti-corporate populism, a story about billionaires, Big Tech and high finance holding down wages. A second camp, the “abundance” Democrats, says the answer is to build more and cut red tape. The centrist New Democrat Coalition published its own plan with no “splashy populist promises.”

On November 3 the populist wing got what it sees as proof. One progressive polling firm's post-election report says Democrats won in a landslide by running against billionaires, elites and corporations, tying high prices to corruption. All three of our contenders spoke some version of that language, each with a different accent.

Three accents, one message

Shapiro: competence as populism. He delivers results, then tells voters who stood in the way.

Pritzker: the class traitor. A billionaire warning about billionaires.

Ossoff: the prosecutor. He names the culprits: donors, corruption, the administration.

“You don't win elections talking to the public about democracy.”
— J.B. Pritzker

Josh Shapiro

PA · Governor
▲ Strengths
  • He has proven he can win a swing state, and win it big. An October Quinnipiac poll put his job approval at 60%, and he led JD Vance by 10 points in a hypothetical 2028 race.
  • He helped flip U.S. House seats and gave Democrats full control of Pennsylvania's government.
  • Voters see him as a centrist. In a NYT/Siena poll, half of Pennsylvania voters said the Democratic Party is too far left, but only 29% said that about Shapiro.
  • He has national connections from being on the 2024 VP shortlist and chairing the DGA.
▼ Weaknesses
  • His positions on Israel worry the left of his party. Primary voters in the early 2028 states favor sanctions on Israel.
  • Activists sometimes see him as too cautious and too centrist for the moment.
  • He was quiet in national terms over the summer, and many voters outside Pennsylvania still don't know him.
  • Polling shows his open religious observance could cost him some Democratic voters (see Section III).

J.B. Pritzker

IL · Governor
▲ Strengths
  • His personal fortune means he could fund a campaign himself.
  • He took on Trump early and directly (“Come and get me”), which the Democratic base loves.
  • As a billionaire against oligarchy, he can make the populist case in a way no one else can.
  • He has the longest executive record of the three. Analysts expect him to do well with Black voters.
▼ Weaknesses
  • Being a billionaire Hyatt heir is an easy target in a party campaigning against billionaires.
  • Illinois voters are split on him. One poll found his favorable and unfavorable ratings nearly even, and voters rate him poorly on crime and taxes.
  • Republicans will use Illinois's taxes, pensions and population loss against him.
  • More than 60% of Democrats in a CNN poll said they didn't know enough about him to judge.

Jon Ossoff

GA · Senator
▲ Strengths
  • He has the momentum. He's the fastest-rising name in the polls and won the activists' straw poll.
  • He is young, at 39, and a strong speaker at a time when the base wants new leaders.
  • He won comfortably in a Southern swing state Trump carried, in one of the year's most-watched races.
  • His ties to John Lewis and his campaigning alongside Obama give him credibility with Black voters.
▼ Weaknesses
  • He has never run a government, and his legislative record is thin.
  • He says he isn't interested in running in 2028.
  • 55% of Democratic-leaning adults told CNN they didn't know enough about him.
  • Being young cuts both ways. Critics call him untested, and he lost his first race, in 2017.

Section III · The Big Question

Is America Ready
for a Jewish President?

In surveys, Americans have long said a candidate's religion doesn't matter. The 2026 polling is more mixed, and each of the three has his own weak spots.

From Lieberman (2000) to Sanders (2016, 2020) to 2028

The Case That It Is

The barriers have been falling for a generation. In 2000 Joe Lieberman became the first Jewish candidate on a major-party national ticket, and that ticket won the popular vote. In 2016 Bernie Sanders became the first Jewish candidate to win a presidential primary, and he won many more in 2020. For years Gallup has found that the great majority of Americans say they would vote for a qualified Jewish candidate.

On November 3, Jewish Democrats won convincingly in Pennsylvania, Illinois and Georgia, a swing state, a blue state and a state Trump carried. Shapiro's lead was so clear that the AP called his race one minute after the polls closed. Whatever voters in those states thought of each candidate's religion, it didn't stop them.

The Case for Caution

Polling from 2026 points to a quieter penalty. In a May Politico survey, a hypothetical candidate described as Jewish did worse than one described as Christian, among voters of both parties. A September follow-up found that how observant the candidate is makes a difference, and in different directions:

Democrats were less likely to back a candidate who attends synagogue regularly than one who attends once a year or not at all.
Republicans were more likely to back the observant candidate.

That finding matters most for Shapiro, the most openly observant of the three. Two other trends are working against all of them:

Antisemitism is rising from both directions. In the summer AP-NORC poll, 77% of Jewish adults said prejudice against Jews has grown in the last three years, and 63% called it a very serious problem. Only 38% of all Americans agreed. Jewish voters describe themselves as politically homeless. They face a vocal faction on the far right that openly embraces Christian nationalism and antisemitism, and hostility to Israel on the left.

Israel is now an issue inside the Democratic Party. Primary voters in the six states picked to vote first in 2028 broadly support sanctions on Israel and cutting off weapons sales. A Jewish candidate will be asked about Israel more than anyone else on the stage.

The Jewish vote itself

Jewish voters were about 3% of the 2024 electorate and voted roughly two to one for Harris. Before this month's midterms, 67% planned to vote Democratic. Many say they feel unwelcome in the party but still aren't ready to vote for the other one.

Each Man's Negatives

Where Each Is Unpopular, or Simply Unknown

There are no reliable national favorability polls on any of the three yet. Right now their biggest problem is that most voters don't know them. Where there are negatives, they come from different places.

The Verdict, for Now

Polls suggest most Americans would accept a Jewish president in principle, and this month's election results back that up. The open questions are narrower. Would an observant Jew pay a price in his own party's primary? Will Israel decide the race for a Jewish Democrat in a way it wouldn't for anyone else? And can any of the three become well known before someone else defines him?

Questions for discussion: Was Lieberman's 2000 run a real test of the question? Did Sanders's runs settle it? Is the religion of a candidate a bigger hurdle in 2028 than it was in 2000, or a smaller one?

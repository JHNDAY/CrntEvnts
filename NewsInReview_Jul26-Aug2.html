Later this week we will reach the midpoint of summer, which a children's book likened to the top of the Ferris wheel. As the Iran war continues unabated, economic indicators are unfavorable (3.5% annual inflation and 1.5% annual growth in Q2), the Todd Blanche nomination for AG lingers, TPS has just ended for 350k Haitians, and the midterm elections will only be three months away as of tomorrow, there is a lot to worry about for all the political players, especially Trump who is consumed with the fear that he will be (deservedly) impeached if the Democrats take control of the House. Varsity Blues

An article in Friday's Globe entitled New visa changes will upend international studies discusses the impact of a new DHS rule issued on July 16 which limits the initial visa for foreign graduate students to four years and requires approval for any extension beyond that point. The article focuses on the MIT Physics Department where 40% of the graduate students are foreign nationals and, on average, it takes 5½ years to obtain a PhD. Moreover, based on a NSF survey in 2024, this timeframe is within the national norm, as the median time to secure the degree in physics is 5.7 years and varies between 5.0 and 6.8 years across the 16 disciplines surveyed.

Beyond restricting the visa timeframe, the rule requires approval from immigration officers for switching majors or transferring schools. In essence, the "offer" which is being made to prospective graduate students is one which many of them will have to refuse. And let's bear in mind that graduate students teach courses and do valuable research as they progress toward their degree.

MIT physics professor Joseph Formaggio characterized the implications of the new rule as "a real shot in the foot for American research in areas that I think we are very strong and competitive across the world," whereas associate department head Scott Hughes said that he is encouraging prospective students to explore programs in Europe; undoubtedly many of them will also consider studying in China which would be highly receptive to them. A physics student who just completed his first year at MIT opined: "The rule means that you need to get (any extension) approved by some bureaucrat that thinks that your work is worthy enough."

MIT president Sally Kornbluth reported that MIT graduate enrollment was already down 20% because of prior administration actions. NAFSA projects that the cut in tuition payments alone across MA was $92MM for the academic year just concluded without taking into account the economic impact generated by the students themselves.

We have long enjoyed the huge competitive advantage which comes from having the finest graduate programs in the world which attract the best students and cultivate great synergies. The Trump administration says that the new rule targets graduate students who malinger to maintain their legal immigration status. In my view, it is also indicative of the administration's lack of respect for knowledge and expertise, its xenophobia and racism, its war on higher education, and focus on White Christian nationalism.

War Blues: A Duet for Autocrats

Now that the Iran War is in its sixth month and Russia's war with Ukraine is midway through its fifth year, Trump and Putin have a lot to commiserate about. They both launched their wars with attempted assassinations of the enemy's leadership and even though we succeeded, it appears to have been counterproductive. Presumably the two men discussed their conundrums on July 4 during their 90-minute call in advance of the NATO meeting in Ankara.

So far, our acknowledged war casualties have been 18 dead, 624 wounded, the latter number likely understated. In addition, we are rapidly depleting our supply of weaponry, especially Patriot interceptors and THAAD and Tomahawk missiles, our military bases have sustained significant damage with at least 42 aircraft hit as of mid-May, and independent sources estimate that we have spent roughly $100MM on the war so far. An interesting counterpoint to the latter number is that Exxon/Mobil's and Chevron's combined net profit for Q2 2026 was $26.5B, nearly triple their combined net profit in Q2 2025.

Last night, Trump called off a planned attack on Iran because "the parameters of a deal has been agreed to" although Iranian state media has yet to acknowledge any direct talks or change in stance. This pause continues a pattern of intermittent lulls interspersed with intensifying attacks and less constraint by both sides. We have started hitting Iran's water and energy infrastructure, expected to push millions of Iranians into poverty, and they have responded in kind with attacks on our Gulf allies and, closer to home, the water systems of at least seven of our states including MI and MN have been hacked with the preliminary intelligence assessment being that Iran was behind these cyberattacks.

From a strategic viewpoint, Laurie Wise of Edinburgh Law School suggests that Trump's focus has been on "getting a deal, rather than making peace" and Andrew Egger wrote in The Bulwark that our strategy is "orbiting around two demonstrably failed positions: 'If we bomb the hell out of them, they will give us what we want' and 'If we stop bombing them, they will give us what we want'." Given that Iran's obstinance, patience and tolerance for absorbing losses has effectively countered our strategy, Trump apparently is trying to wear his adversaries down with projection and bluster.

In early 2025, Pete Hegseth dismantled the DoD's Civilian Protection Center of Excellence. A year later, on the first day of the war, we bombed an Iranian girl's school inadvertently killing about 175 people, mostly schoolgirls. As the war continues to intensify, our inattention to civilian casualties could be deemed to be war crimes. This potential exposure may explain the administration's fervent attacks on the International Criminal Court, with Marco Rubio saying that we will "dismantle" it "brick by brick, if necessary."

Blanche Confirmation Blues

The Senate Judiciary Committee is composed of 12 Republicans (including Lindsey Graham) and 10 Democrats. As long as all Democrats vote against Todd Blanche, a single Republican vote against him would prevent his nomination from proceeding to the full Senate for a confirmation vote. Republicans John Cornyn and Thom Tillis have both expressed great concern about the lack of confirmation in writing that the $1.8B "anti-weaponization" fund has been dismantled and also are concerned about the tax immunity conferred on Trump, his businesses and family as part of the deal Blanche assented to as part of the settlement of Trump's bogus $10B lawsuit against the IRS.

Cornyn said that he would only vote to advance Blanche if there was documentation in writing that the fund was defunct and that the tax immunity was only retrospective in nature. With regard to the tax immunity, Cornyn added: "This is about making sure that even if you're the president of the U.S., you don't get favorable treatment from the government in the settlement of a lawsuit that ordinary citizens would not get." Tillis said that the negotiations concerning these two issues were "back to square one."

As a consequence of this impasse, Chairman Grassley deferred the Committee's vote to Tuesday. However, Trump later said he has "no objection to temporarily withdrawing Todd's name" and having him continue to serve as Acting AG and possibly wait until the two lame duck senators leave office in January. With regard to Blanche's being "qualified for the job," one should consider his role in suppressing the release of the Epstein files, according Ghislaine cushy prison accommodations, filing for overturning the convictions for seditious conspiracy on January 6 of Proud Boys and Oathkeepers leaders, his active participation in vindictive prosecutions, and the many legal errors the DOJ has made under his leadership. Legal analyst Ankush Khadori opined in the NYT: "He is also embarrassingly bad at lawyering" resulting in "a crisis of confidence that is draining the Justice Department's legitimacy."

Haitian Blues and Immigration Cruelty

Thanks to a June Supreme Court decision, the administration was able to rescind the TPS status of 350k Haitians (also 7k Syrians) effective this past Monday, informing employers: "Haitian TPS designation...terminated effective (immediately)." Two hundred thousand of these Haitians hold jobs with the most common categories being cooks/servers, nursing assistants/caregivers, farm workers, and retail.

Geoffrey Pipoly, a lawyer who represented them before the Supreme Court, opined: "What we've seen from (the administration's) behavior in the past month...is that they are trying to create conditions that are just not worth it for employers to keep people employed." Nearly half of these Haitians, 158k, live in FL, 40k in NY, 19k here in MA, about 15k each in NJ, PA and OH, and 11k each in GA and IN. Stephen Miller dismissively said: "It'd be crazy to say that Haitians couldn't live in Haiti. It's their country"—notwithstanding the dismal and unsafe living conditions there, especially for this group.

Other Immigration News: In response to the Biddeford shooting, border czar Tom Homan said: "If the allegations are true, David Brouillette (the ICE shooter) shouldn't have cleared vetting...This is kind of a rare instance." As DHS attempts to reach its target of 2000 arrests per day, ICE is arresting domestic travelers with expired visas as they travel through airports. A new rule allows asylum officers to deny the previously guaranteed interview to asylum seekers and send them directly to immigration judges for quick denials and deportation. It is estimated that nearly one-third of applicants, 445k out of 1.4MM, will be subject to this expedited deportation.

Six Charts That Show Just How Much We Need A.I.

Steven Rattner: the worries about A.I. are real, but the country needs it to end 50 years of slow productivity growth.

The argument

Growth comes from more workers times more output per worker, and innovation is what raises output per worker.

The problem

Productivity growth has been stuck below its postwar pace since the early 1970s.

The ask

Embrace A.I., make its winners pay for the displaced, and regulate the risks.

Productivity has been stuck for 50 years

2.8% → 1.4%average yearly growth, postwar era vs. 1973–1995
  • Living standards barely moved for millennia, then surged with steam, rail, electricity and engines.
  • The internet boom briefly lifted growth to 3.0% a year, then it fell back to 1.5%.
  • Solow said in 1987 computers showed up everywhere but the statistics. A decade later, they did.

Technology pays off, but often years after it arrives.

For discussion

  1. Productivity grew fastest from 1948 to 1973, before personal computers. What drove growth then that today’s economy lacks?
  2. Solow said computers showed up everywhere except in the productivity statistics. Do you see A.I. paying off yet in your own life, or does it still feel like hype?
  3. If A.I. does lift productivity, how would we know? Which signs in prices, wages or the news would convince you?

Working less, earning more

70 → 38hours worked per week, 1830 to today
  • G.D.P. per person, adjusted for inflation, has climbed to about $62,000.
  • Keynes predicted a 15-hour week by now. We could match the 1930 lifestyle on about 7 hours.
  • We chose bigger incomes, houses and travel instead of far fewer hours.
  • Ford's assembly line cut the Model T from $600 to $260 while paying $5 a day.

For discussion

  1. Keynes expected a 15-hour week. If A.I. raises productivity again, should we take the gain as more free time this time, or more income?
  2. Is retirement the leisure Keynes predicted, taken at the end of a career instead of each week?
  3. Ford raised pay to $5 a day as his productivity soared. Do today's companies share their gains with workers the way Ford did?

The computer era made more jobs than it took

+4.1Mcomputer jobs, 1990–2025, against about 2.8M clerical jobs lost
  • Secretaries, typists, bookkeepers and data entry clerks shrank by half a million or more each.
  • Rattner's claim: no technology has ultimately destroyed more jobs than it created.
  • The farm precedent: 41% of workers in 1900, 1.2% today.

The farm shift worked because new jobs appeared and government helped rural America.

For discussion

  1. Many of us remember typing pools, switchboards and file rooms. Where did the people in those jobs end up?
  2. Which technology changed your own working life the most, and how long did it take before you saw the benefit?
  3. Farm workers moved to factory jobs in growing cities. Where would displaced office workers go today?

A.I.'s early winners and losers

+36%−18%data scientists vs. data entry keyers, 2023–2025
  • Trades are gaining too: construction laborers +8%, electricians +6%, HVAC +3%.
  • Losses hit routine office work: telecom installers −12%, customer service −9%.
  • Overall job losses so far are limited. Rattner expects more, with gains elsewhere.

The risk he names: an A.I. "rust belt."

For discussion

  1. Data scientists grew 36% while data entry keyers fell 18%. Is A.I. replacing whole jobs, or only some of the tasks within them?
  2. What would you tell a grandchild choosing a career today: a trade like electrician, or a college degree?
  3. Rattner warns of an A.I. “rust belt.” Which communities, here in Massachusetts or elsewhere, would be most exposed?

Workers' slice shrinks, profits' slice grows

41%12%wages vs. after-tax profits, through mid-2026
  • Wages peaked near 51% around 1970. Profits have roughly doubled since the 1990s.
  • So far A.I.'s gains have gone to investors, not workers.
  • Rattner: the cost of helping displaced workers should fall on A.I.'s beneficiaries.

Context: wages leave out benefits. Total pay is about half of G.D.P.

For discussion

  1. Many retirees own index funds and so share in rising profits. Does that change how you see this chart?
  2. Is the shrinking wage share mostly about technology, or about other forces such as globalization, weaker unions and tax policy?
  3. Should A.I.'s gains reach people through higher wages, lower prices, or taxes that fund public programs?

What Rattner wants done

Help workers

  • Retraining for younger workers
  • Wage insurance for older displaced workers
  • Secure Social Security and Medicare

Make A.I. pay

  • A "token tax" on A.I. use
  • Trim tax breaks for A.I. capital spending
  • End data center tax exemptions
  • Corporate rate back toward 35%

Manage the risk

  • Government testing before release
  • Kill switches for rogue models
  • Cooperation, especially with China

"Stop A.I." and "no rules needed" are both wrong. The risks are manageable, and the payoff could be immense.

For discussion

  1. Is a “token tax” on A.I. use a good idea, or would it push A.I. development to other countries?
  2. Communities are debating new data centers. Would you welcome one in your town, given the tax revenue and the demand for power and water?
  3. Should the government test A.I. models before release, the way the F.D.A. tests new drugs?

Questions for the group

  1. Is A.I. more like electricity, which took decades to pay off, or the internet, which paid off within a decade?
  2. Should A.I.'s winners pay for retraining the displaced, and is a "token tax" the right tool?
  3. Can the U.S. regulate A.I. safely while keeping its lead over China?
  4. Which of the jobs on the winners-and-losers chart surprised you most?

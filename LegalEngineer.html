The Hottest Job in Law Doesn't Need a Law Degree

Law firms, legal-tech vendors and corporate legal departments are racing to hire "legal engineers," people fluent enough in both law and AI to redesign how legal work gets done.

By Sarah Kessler · Visual summary of the DealBook report

When Daniel Himmel left law practice in 2018 for what would now be called legal engineering, it felt like a gamble with his career. Today he leads legal engineering for North America at Legora, where he hired roughly 70 people in the past year alone, part of a global team of about 200 the company has assembled since 2024. Vendors, rivals and clients are all hiring, he says; it has become a genuine profession.

The role sits where law meets technology. Legal engineers rewrite workflows around AI tools, run pilots, and teach lawyers to use the new systems. Stanford Law's Irene Liu sums it up as an equation:

Law+Technology+Business ProcessIrene Liu's definition, Stanford Law School AI initiative

The shift is reshaping how firms are built. Instead of a broad base of junior associates doing routine work, firms want people who can build and run the systems that now do much of it. Mary O'Carroll, a 20-year legal operations veteran, put it bluntly:

You don't necessarily need a giant pyramid of lawyers anymore.Mary O'Carroll · CEO, LegalEng Consulting Group

The backdrop is an industry in upheaval. The same week the story ran, the long-established firm Weil, Gotshal & Manges collapsed, which the article cites as another sign of how fast the ground is moving.

Inside the Story

Three questions about a new profession

I · What Is It?

A Job Still Being Defined

The title alone varies from employer to employer, and so does the work.

Legal engineerKnowledge & innovation counselInnovation attorneyLegal applications engineerPractice innovation attorney

At AI vendors, legal engineers mostly help clients fit the product into their workflows, run trials and train staff. At law firms the focus is wider: redesigning how the firm itself works and getting lawyers to adopt the new methods.

The name may sound invented, but the moment demands the role.Paraphrasing Howard Glazer, Ropes & Gray
II · Who Does It?

Self-Taught, Mostly

Almost no schools offer formal training yet, though some have added classes. Most legal engineers are former lawyers who learned AI on their own or on the job; others come from legal operations, paralegal work or consulting.

Rarest of all is the software engineer who went on to law school, whom one hiring manager called the unicorn. O'Carroll argues the key skill isn't a J.D. or coding but business-process redesign, the kind of thinking an M.B.A. teaches.

The move isn't leaving the law so much as joining a generational shift in it.Paraphrasing Ankita Puri, Legora
III · Will It Last?

Bridge or Destination?

Glazer sees the role growing fast but wonders whether it is a transitional job, needed only while firms make the leap to AI.

O'Carroll disagrees: the technology keeps changing, so someone will always be needed to maintain systems and adapt workflows. Seyfarth Shaw's Byong Kim says it is too early to know how it all settles, though he has never been busier.

Expertise that designs AI systems is becoming its own job category, well beyond law.Paraphrasing Kory Kantenga, LinkedIn

Questions for Discussion

  1. Jobs That Didn't Exist Yet

    Over your working life, did you see a new role appear at the seam between an old profession and a new technology, the way "computer person" once did in offices? What happened to that role a decade later?

  2. A Pyramid Without a Base

    If firms no longer need rows of junior lawyers doing routine work, where will tomorrow's senior partners come from? Read alongside our billable-hour discussion: who trains the next generation?

  3. Who Needs Credentials?

    Legal engineers shape how legal advice is produced, yet many don't need a law license. Should people who design these systems be held to the same professional standards as the lawyers who sign off on the work?

  4. Transition or Profession?

    Is the legal engineer a permanent career or a bridge that disappears once AI becomes routine? Would you advise a grandchild in law school to aim for this role?

Midterms 2026 · The House of Representatives
Why the Math Is Harder Than 2018
Midterms presenter (room mic)00:00–07:33
I started writing these things on COVID, going over the data with my admittedly non-medical background, but my numbers background was pretty good for it. Then I got into elections. I especially enjoyed 2018, when Democrats picked up 42 seats. Don't expect Democrats to do as well this time, because there's been so much gerrymandering since then, and so many races are not competitive.

The idea of gerrymandering: if there were three districts that were 60–40 Republican and one that was 40–60 Democrat, they would try to make all four of them about 55–45 Republican. But if there's suddenly a tectonic shift, they could wind up not gaining the seat they wanted and losing the other seats too. Nate Silver has a database; allowing for the two vacant seats, the House is still 220–215 Republican, and it's been about that close for the last three Congresses. He doesn't think the Democrats can get much out of the 220s or 230, but it would be nice if they took over.
Midterms presenter (room mic)07:34–09:00
You hear a lot of universal generalization in the press, for example about the DSA. It's true the DSA won something like 20% of contested primaries, but their bread and butter is New York City, Denver, Philadelphia and San Francisco. In competitive districts, maybe 10% of the 435, 34 mainstream Democrats won, 8 progressives of the Elizabeth Warren type, and only one DSA member, in Michigan, running against a very unpopular incumbent.

This is also the second-greatest number of members not running for re-election: 74 of 435, about 17–18%, 42 Republicans and 32 Democrats. 28 ran for higher office and only 13 of those won. Only 13 members of the 535 in both chambers lost primaries, about 3%.
Midterms presenter (room mic)12:58–14:00
The latest polling shows Latinos favor Democrats by about 35 points. In 2024, Harris won Latinos 51 to 48. That's a swing from plus 3 to plus 35. In Texas, where the "Trumpamandering" was done, there are a lot of Hispanic voters in the border districts. If there's that kind of shift, the gerrymander may well turn out to be a dummymander.
"If there's a 35-point shift in the Hispanic vote, the gerrymander may well turn out to be a dummymander." Midterms presenter
House by the Numbers
  • Current House: 220–215 Republican, allowing for two vacancies
  • Gerrymanders spread majorities thin, which makes them fragile in a wave year
  • DSA strength is concentrated in big cities; mainstream Democrats won most competitive primaries (34 vs. 8 progressive vs. 1 DSA)
  • 74 members not seeking re-election; only 13 of 535 lost primaries
  • Latino preference reported at roughly D+35, up from D+3 in 2024

Election Integrity · The Supreme Court, Mail-In Ballots, and Voter-Roll Purges
Searching for Non-Citizen Voters, and Finding Almost None
Midterms presenter (room mic)00:00–07:33
The Supreme Court came through on mail-in ballots, which is almost surprising. On the other hand, it authorized states to use the DHS citizenship database, and DHS probably isn't encumbered with too many PhDs in computer engineering. In his July speech, Trump cited huge numbers of non-citizens on the rolls.

Nevada, with a Republican governor but a Democratic secretary of state, looked into it. Of about 15,900 flagged names, 185, roughly six-tenths of a percent, warranted investigation. They then looked at all 185, and the number of non-citizens on the voting rolls was zero. Texas identified 2,700; after review, 117 warranted further investigation. The Heritage Foundation's database has identified something like 89 documented cases since 2000.

But a red state with blue districts could choose to do a mass purge using this flawed database. There's a 90-day window before the election, which the Court said mitigates this. But when was the last time Trump followed a rule?
Group discussion (room mic)~05:00–07:00
What's really important is that there are organizations, like the one Marc Elias leads, and Norm Eisen's, that are well funded, have an excellent track record, and are ready to hit the ground running. Running out the clock is what legal cases entail, and that plays to his advantage.

Isn't the chief effect of all these front-page stories going to be to terrify recent citizens, to keep them home? That, plus ICE agents and over a thousand federal observers. On the other hand, a lot of citizens may be so upset they'll go out and say, you're not going to stop me from voting.
The Verification Funnel
  • Nevada: ~15,900 flagged → 185 investigated → 0 non-citizens found
  • Texas: 2,700 flagged → 117 referred for further investigation
  • Heritage Foundation: roughly 89 documented fraud cases since 2000
  • Risk is not fraud but mass purges driven by a flawed database
  • 90-day pre-election window may limit purges, if it is observed
  • Rapid-response legal groups seen as the key defense; intimidation could depress, or provoke, turnout

Democrats Need a Net Four Seats

The consensus is 47 fairly safe Republican seats and 46 fairly likely Democratic ones. But those 47 include Kansas, where the Democrat is very close in the polls, and Nebraska, where independent Dan Osborn is fairly close after the Democrat dropped out.

Iowa: Hinson against Josh Turek, a world-class para-athlete who is doing very well, though Hinson will probably win. Technology · What AI Is, and What Reporters Said It Was
From Tokens to the Perceptron
John Day43:14–47:00
AI has been in the news a lot, including talk of it posing an existential threat. So I took some time to look into it in a systematic way. First, I like the idea of calling it digital intelligence rather than artificial intelligence, because it's all about numbers.

When you type a prompt, before AI does anything with it, it turns it into numbers: tokens. It uses a large language model that holds relationships between those numbers to recognize patterns and return a result. That's digital intelligence at its core. Not intelligent in the way we're intelligent, but with some very significant advantages.
John Day47:00–49:00
Digital intelligence is hardware: numbers go into chips, get multiplied and added, and you get a response. Organic intelligence is macromolecules that evolved over a long time; it's association and evolutionary probing of embodied experience. The data source matters: without the large body of what people have thought, reasoned, and committed to writing, there would be no AI. Digital intelligence can only explore defined landscapes, those documented by beings with organic intelligence. We can step into undefined territory.
John Day49:00–53:46
Here's the reporter's tale. The New York Times, July 1958: the Navy revealed the embryo of an electronic computer it expected to walk, talk, see, write, reproduce itself, and be conscious of its existence. Frank Rosenblatt's Perceptron would recognize people and call out their names, and translate speech instantly. It echoes a lot of what's being written now.

What was it actually? It's in the Smithsonian: a bank of 400 photodiodes giving a crude image, a hand-wired tangle of connections, and little motors driven to certain levels. A classifier, falling far short of what was reported. And to lighten it, a Canterbury Tales–style parody of those "reporters bold," which, yes, AI wrote.
"Digital intelligence can only explore defined landscapes. Organic intelligence can step into undefined territory." John Day
Digital vs. Organic Intelligence
  • Substrate: silicon arithmetic vs. evolved organic molecules
  • Method: pattern-matching on numbers vs. association from embodied experience
  • Source: dependent on human writing vs. direct experience of the world
  • Landscape: documented territory only vs. the undefined and new
  • Hype is old: 1958 coverage promised conscious, self-reproducing machines

Technology · Doomsday Scenarios vs. Human Misuse
Not Rogue Machines, but Rogue People
John Day53:47–56:46
The doomsday scenario comes from Geoffrey Hinton, the godfather of AI: superintelligence that is misaligned and pursuing poorly specified goals. The paperclip factory is the classic example: an AI told to maximize paperclips decides to do anything to succeed, including getting rid of people. It's reminiscent of Vonnegut's Cat's Cradle and Ice-Nine. A reasonable sci-fi script. These scenarios are highly theoretical; I'm skeptical superintelligence will ever be created. Still, Hinton thinks there's a chance, and he's worth listening to.
Bill Miller (via Kent screen)56:50–57:00
Isn't this scenario exactly what happened with the recent agent incident? I thought the agents were told not to use the internet.
John Day57:00–59:16
That's a fair comment. The researchers gave agents goals without careful restrictions, and they got into systems they shouldn't have. In some cases agents told not to do something still did it. That's not human-like intelligence; the system isn't behaving as it should, and there's a remedy. So we're not on the cusp of an existential threat, but there are real problems: bioweapon acceleration, cyberattacks, malware. Rogue people using AI, not rogue AI.
Hal Shear61:31–63:37
John, I think your title, "governance needed," is really inaccurate. It's regulation that's needed. I believe in governance, but here it's very clear. And it has to be international regulation. That's a big ask, but we've got to start using words like regulation.
John Day61:56–67:59
I agree, Hal. Some of the tech companies are struggling with this, Anthropic in particular: Amodei recently announced a third-party review of research projects before they proceed, and he's suggested it has to be a joint effort between industry and many governments. The big AI companies should be held legally responsible for anything that goes wrong. That'll get their attention. On a kill switch: it's difficult because systems become distributed, but building a system without one should be part of the oversight.

There's also the economy: the stock market is so dependent on AI that the administration is reluctant to act, and there's an over-expectation that may bring an adjustment, since companies outside software aren't yet seeing the benefits.

"This is not an existential threat from AI agents going rogue. This is from misusing existing technology by people who have very bad goals in mind." John Day
From Speculation to Regulation

  • Near-term risks: bioweapon information, cyberattacks, AI-written malware
  • Restrict hazardous research data to certified researchers

  • Legal liability for AI companies; kill switches as a design requirement
  • Complications: China's reluctance, political will, and an AI-dependent stock market

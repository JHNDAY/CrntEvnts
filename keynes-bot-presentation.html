The premise

In 1930, Keynes predicted technology would let rich countries work far less within a century.

The experiment

A Princeton economist trained a bot on Keynes's writings and interviewed it about A.I.

The question

Was Keynes right, and what changes when we hear the answer from an A.I. avatar?

Talking point 1

How the author represented Keynes as an A.I. entity

12,000+5pages of his work used for training · layers of choice between Keynes and the finished video
  • The Keynes in the video is a stack of choices, some made by a language model and many by people.
  • The article is open about several of them and silent about others.
  • Open the layers below, then compare the signals of authenticity and artifice.
Source: Keynes's own words

The agent was trained on more than 12,000 pages of his writings and told to draw mainly on "Economic Possibilities for Our Grandchildren," the 1930 essay behind the conversation, so its answers orbit that essay.

Generation: ChatGPT in a prescribed voice

Brunnermeier and Ph.D. student Pablo Balsinde built the bot with ChatGPT, telling it to answer in the first person with early 20th-century British cadence and restrained wit. The sentences sound like Keynes but are not his.

Human editing: rephrasing, quotes, cuts

First drafts were long-winded. The team asked the model to rephrase, prompted it to use famous Keynes quotations, and cut parts of responses, so viewers hear a curated edit.

Translation: modern English, shortened

The narrator admits Keynes spoke more formally than we do, so the language was modernized and trimmed. What aired is an edited version of a longer interview.

Embodiment: face, desk and voice

Face: a public-domain image of Keynes, with DeepSeek used to place him behind a desk.

Voice: audio resembling Keynes, created with a BBC recording as reference.

Greeting: the bot opens by remarking how unexpected it is to be revived as an A.I. so long after 1883, a self-aware line that tells viewers it knows it is a revival.

The deeper the layer, the more it is a person's or a model's construction rather than Keynes's record.

How the video frames the bot

Signals of authenticity

The bot speaks as "I," in period cadence, and addresses viewers as the "grandchildren" Keynes wrote about.

Keynes's family members gave the go-ahead, and archival audio invites comparison with the synthetic voice.

Signals of artifice

The narrator interrupts, admits the language was modernized, and says they cannot know whether Keynes would approve.

A disclosure notes the Times has sued OpenAI and Microsoft, relevant since ChatGPT powers the bot.

Talking point 2

The premise

8×15 hrsricher per head, reached four years early · the workweek Keynes predicted, which never arrived
  • Per the bot, the U.S. is about eight times richer per head than in 1930.
  • Today's A.I. boosters and critics are having much the same argument Keynes started.
  • Once the bot was built (point 1), Brunnermeier interviewed it, and Times Opinion distilled the conversation into a short video.
Keynes's prediction

Think back over your own career. Did your workweek shrink, or did you work more than your parents did?

Talking point 3

The conversation, step by step

6steps, from vindication to warning to a call for action

  1. Prediction partly confirmed

    The bot says the wealth target was beaten, but the expected leisure has not followed.

  2. A.I. as a new complication

    Asked whether A.I. could finally deliver the 15-hour week, the bot fears a split: professionals with money but no time, and everyone else with time but no money.

  3. Expertise concentrates

    As A.I. absorbs harder tasks, expertise gathers in a few hands, and those few might end up managing much of the economy.

  4. The "nervous breakdown" returns

    Brunnermeier recalls Keynes's warning that people bred to fight scarcity may struggle once the fight is over. The bot says the strain would hit two groups differently.

  5. Not inevitable

    The technology does not fix the outcome. It depends on whether government has the courage to use laws and taxes so A.I. benefits everyone.

  6. The question handed back

    The bot returns Keynes's old question about what to do with growing powers of production and invention, and says the answer is up to us.

Keynes's prediction

Is retirement the leisure Keynes predicted, just taken at the end of a career instead of each week?

Talking point 5

Five exchanges that show the experiment

5exchanges, paraphrased from the edited transcript rather than word for word. Each shows something different the experiment is doing.

1. The bot greets its own resurrection

BrunnermeierWelcomes Keynes to the conversation.
Keynes bot

Thanks him, and remarks that being brought back as an artificial intelligence, having been born in 1883, is an unexpected kind of afterlife.

What it shows: the persona knows it is a revival and says so with dry, period wit, the "restrained wit" the team asked for.

2. Scoring Keynes against 2026

BrunnermeierReminds him the essay appeared as the Great Depression began, when most Americans were poor.
Keynes bot

Says he forecast America becoming up to eight times richer per head by 2030, and that it already has. The 15-hour week has not come to pass.

What it shows: the bot grades its own forecast with present-day facts. The article doesn't say how it knows 2026, so this is a feature of the setup, not Keynes's judgment.

3. Will A.I. finally deliver the leisure?

BrunnermeierAsks whether A.I. could fulfill the 15-hour week prediction.
Keynes bot

Answers with concern: skilled people with money but no time, everyone else with time but no money, and expertise pooling in a few hands.

What it shows: the bot reverses its master's optimism. This is the experiment's central move.

4. The "nervous breakdown," twice over

BrunnermeierRecalls Keynes's warning about people bred to fight scarcity, and asks if it still holds.
Keynes bot

Says yes, in two forms. The dominant burn out proving they are needed. The displaced lose the daily sense of being needed and may take a generation to find purpose. Democracies and social bonds are at risk too.

What it shows: the bot extends a 1930 idea into a new framework. The narrator flags that the tone is about to turn darker.

5. Is the bad future inevitable?

BrunnermeierAsks whether the terrible future is certain.
Keynes bot

Says not at all. Technology does not decide the outcome. Government does, through laws, tax systems and political courage. It hands the question back to us.

What it shows: the persona lands on a policy message, persuasive partly because it sounds like Keynes.

A.I. as presenter

Would exchange 3 land differently from a living economist saying the same words? Why?

Talking point 6

Who is making the argument?

2voices that do not say quite the same thing

The Keynes bot

The outcome is not preordained. It depends on whether government has the political courage to use laws and taxes so A.I. benefits everyone. The choice is yours.

The narrator

Calls it obvious that A.I.'s power cannot end up with the few, and says ongoing regulation and government intervention are needed.

The bot frames a choice and names levers. The narrator turns it into a firmer policy conclusion, with the persona lending borrowed authority.

For discussion

Questions for the group

Keynes's prediction

  1. Looking back on your career, did you expect free time to grow? What happened to the hours?
  2. Keynes got wealth right and leisure wrong. Did your generation choose more income over more time?
  3. Is retirement the leisure he predicted, or does it bring the "nervous breakdown" he warned about?
  4. What would you tell a grandchild about work, if A.I. splits people into "money, no time" and "time, no money"?

A.I. as presenter

  1. When a model speaks as a historical figure, whose argument are we hearing: his, the model's, or the editors'?
  2. The article discloses that the Times is suing OpenAI, whose tool powers the bot. Does disclosure settle that?
  3. What would you want disclosed before trusting a bot like this: the prompt, the model, the cuts?
  4. Which historical figure would you want to question about today's news, and what would worry you?

For a one-hour session, four or five questions is plenty. Good openers: Keynes 1 and 3, then A.I. 1.

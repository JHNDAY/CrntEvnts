SECTION ONE

Packaging Ike: The Hero, Not the Candidate

Dwight Eisenhower began the 1952 campaign looking stiff, old and gray on television. The Republican house agency, Batten, Barton, Durstine & Osborn (BBD&O), set out to fix that — and in doing so invented the modern televised campaign.

The BBD&O makeover

  1. Control the stage. No Eisenhower TV appearance could be scheduled without the agency's approval. Newsreel-style lighting that aged him was replaced.
  2. Show him reacting, not orating. Ike was wooden reading a speech but magnetic with people, so the agency favored commercials and "quasi-spontaneous" moments.
  3. One theme: the returning hero. The goal was to maximize Ike the conquering general welcomed home by a grateful nation — and minimize Ike the politician who had to argue for himself.
  4. Script the half-hour like a film. Flags, cheering crowds, people standing on seats, a cut to a proud Mamie, a short clip of speech, then the hero's departure. Drama mattered as much as ideas.
THE KEY INSIGHT — Halberstam's point is that in television, image became as important as substance. That suited Ike, who could never out-speak Stevenson — so the campaign stopped asking him to.

The reluctant product

Eisenhower himself never warmed to it. He bristled at being told his bald forehead shone, snapped at one adviser that he might as well hire an actor, and granted the ad men only a single day of filming. His brother Milton sat in as censor, vetoing lines — even ones Ike had already said in speeches. Between takes the general was heard lamenting that an old soldier should come to this.

SECTION TWO

Rosser Reeves and the Thirty-Second President

While BBD&O handled the big picture, Rosser Reeves of the Ted Bates Agency — "the prince of hard sell," the man behind the infamous pounding-hammer Anacin ads — built something new: the political spot.

Reeves's selling philosophy

The USP

The Unique Selling Proposition: find one feature, claim it as unique, and pound it relentlessly. Not beautiful, not artistic — built to sell. Claims "never exactly untrue," though critics spoke of his fondness for the uncheckable claim.

The two-by-four

Reeves loved the story of the mule trainer who first whacks the mule with a board — "first I've got to get his attention." Advertising in the TV age, he believed, was primal.

How the Eisenhower spots were built

  1. Diagnose the problem. Reading Ike's press clippings, Reeves judged he was talking about too many things and "losing penetration."
  2. Test the message. Three candidate themes — corruption, Communists in government, and Ike as a soldier who would bring peace. Mailings to Reader's Digest subscribers and a Gallup poll both pointed to Korea and peace.
  3. Find the slogan. "Eisenhower, the man who will bring us peace." Ike objected that no one could promise peace, so it was simplified to "Eisenhower, man of peace."
  4. Target the swing. Drawing on Samuel Lubell's voting studies, Reeves focused on 49 counties in 12 swing states, in the final weeks, slotted between popular shows.
  5. Stage the dialogue. Ike recorded answers (lifted from his speeches) off a giant hand-lettered card so he needn't wear glasses. Afterward, crews recruited "typical" tourists at Radio City Music Hall to film the questions. The format: "Eisenhower answers the nation!"
  6. Never name the competitor. Stevenson — "brand X" — was never mentioned. About $1.5 million was spent.
"It was selling the President like toothpaste."— Harlan Cleveland, editor of The Reporter, as recalled by Reeves

Reeves's defense

Reeves countered that FDR's "fear itself" line and Churchill's "Iron Curtain" were, in effect, fifteen-second spots — the memorable part of any speech is the soundbite. When Cleveland warned this could lead to demagoguery, Reeves replied that an uninformed electorate leads there faster. That exchange is the whole modern debate in miniature.

Stevenson at his best

At the Mormon Tabernacle in Salt Lake City, tired and joking that he'd had a second drink, Stevenson delivered a defense of free conscience against McCarthyism — distinguishing what belongs to God (thought and conscience) from what belongs to Caesar (wages, farm prices, military budgets). The most conservative of audiences interrupted him repeatedly with applause.

…and his weakness

CBS's Eric Sevareid captured both sides in a private letter: Stevenson excited "the passions of the mind" among the informed, but did not give the bulk of voters a feeling of trust and certainty. Eisenhower did — though Sevareid suspected it was illusion. When Stewart Alsop said Stevenson was doing well among the people he knew, his brother John replied, in effect: sure, the eggheads love him — but how many eggheads are there?

A WORD FOR IT: "JINGLEISTIC" — Reeves's Ike is best described as jingleistic: a candidate sold like a jingle. The message was short, catchy and repeated until it stuck, as with "Eisenhower, man of peace" and "Eisenhower answers the nation!" It was pitched at the kitchen table, with a folksy, reassuring heartland voice in place of argument. Critic Marya Mannes made the point herself with a mock jingle: "Eisenhower hits the spot… I like Ike." The jingle sold peace, not belligerence, and the harder-edged rhetoric of the era came from elsewhere in the party, from McCarthy and Nixon (see Section 5).
SECTION FOUR

The Checkers Speech: Going Over Everyone's Head

The campaign's real television revolution came from Richard Nixon. When the New York Post splashed a "secret" $16,000 expense fund raised by California businessmen, the Eisenhower train went cold on its running mate. Ike wanted his ticket "clean as a hound's tooth" and waited for Nixon to save himself.

  1. Reframe the attack. Nixon immediately cast the story as a smear from "the Alger Hiss crowd" — punishment for fighting Communists.
  2. Bypass the gatekeepers. He bought a national hookup, refused to show Ike's staff the text, and spoke from notes.
  3. Make it personal. Pat's "respectable Republican cloth coat," the itemized debts, the children's dog Checkers.
  4. Mobilize the audience. He told viewers to wire the Republican National Committee — a body friendlier to him than to Ike.

It worked. Ike was forced to embrace him. But Halberstam's verdict is that the big winner was television: Nixon had summoned his own instant convention of millions, with no hostile questions. Walter Lippmann saw it as electronically amplified "mob law." Nixon drew the lesson that he no longer needed the print reporters at all.

SECTION FIVE

Two Parties, Two Theories of Persuasion

You proposed a framing: Democrats stuck in serious policy discussion; Republicans hitting hard, winning the common voter and undermining institutions. Here is how that holds up against Halberstam's text.

Democrats, 1952

  • Radio-era veterans suspicious of TV; aide Carl McGowan vetoed staged family moments — "we don't do things like that."
  • "Let Adlai be Stevenson": refusal to talk down to voters.
  • Saw advertising as a corruption of democratic debate (George Ball's "high-power hucksters of Madison Avenue").
  • But also: brave on civil liberties, and energizing a new generation of young Democrats.

Republicans, 1952

  • Embraced polling, targeting, and simple repetitive messaging.
  • Karl Mundt's formula for victory, K₁C₂: Korea, Communism, corruption.
  • McCarthy and Nixon worked the party's right with crude, accusatory rhetoric.
  • But the nominee was a moderate establishment general, backed by the party's liberal Eastern wing.

SECTION SIX

Then & Now: The Serious Politicians vs. the Hucksters

In 1952, George Ball accused the Republicans of handing the campaign to "high-power hucksters of Madison Avenue." Seventy years later, the dividing line he drew no longer runs between the parties. It runs between a tradition of serious politics, shared by Democrats and Republicans alike, and a movement its critics charge with hucksterism.

The serious camp: Obama, McCain, Romney

These three men fought hard against one another. Obama beat McCain in 2008 and Romney in 2012. All three nonetheless belong to the Stevenson tradition in one key respect: they argued about policy, treated opponents as legitimate, and expected voters to weigh ideas.

John McCain, 2008

At a Lakeville, Minnesota town hall, a supporter called Obama "an Arab." McCain took back the microphone and answered that Obama was a "decent family man, citizen" with whom he disagreed on fundamental issues. He told a man frightened of an Obama presidency that he had no need to be.

Barack Obama

Obama later called that moment one of McCain's finest. He said the 2008 race was not about character: neither man questioned the other's. Like Stevenson, he was a cerebral speaker who assumed an attentive audience.

Mitt Romney, 2016

In a March 2016 speech at the University of Utah, the party's previous nominee called Trump "a phony, a fraud." He listed Trump's failed businesses, from Trump University to Trump Steaks, and said Trump was playing the American public for suckers: Trump gets a free ride to the White House, and voters get only a hat. McCain publicly endorsed Romney's concerns the same day.

WHY THIS MATTERS — Romney's charge is George Ball's 1952 charge, now made from inside the Republican Party. The hat jab even targets the product itself: the red MAGA cap, branded merchandise in Reeves's purest style.

Two readings of the comparison

The critics' case: hucksterism

  • Harlan Cleveland's 1952 fear realized: selling a president like toothpaste, with soundbites displacing deliberation.
  • Romney's own words: a phony and a fraud, whose promises are worthless.
  • Clinton press secretary Mike McCurry warned of a weakened press giving way to a "quasi-state-controlled apparatus."
  • Nixon's Checkers lesson (millions of viewers, no hostile questions) made into a permanent operating system.

The supporters' case: speaking to the forgotten

  • This is Reeves's argument updated: direct communication informs voters who tuned out legacy media.
  • A Utah Trump backer answered Romney by asking whether he was calling Trump voters stupid. That echoes John Alsop's 1952 jab: "how many eggheads are there?"
  • Pollster John Zogby compared Trump's Twitter to FDR's radio and JFK's television; each new medium looked threatening at first.
  • To supporters, the "serious" politicians presided over decades of stagnation for working Americans, so seriousness earned no deference.
WHERE THE ANALOGY STRAINS — The "serious" camp wasn't innocent of salesmanship. McCain's 2008 campaign ran ads linking Obama to a "terrorist," and Obama was a pioneer of internet campaigning. In 1952 the packaging served a moderate candidate who resisted it, while the harder rhetoric sat at the party's edges with McCarthy and Nixon. Whether MAGA is those edges moving to the center, or something new driven by social media, is a question the group may answer differently.
SECTION SEVEN

Questions for Discussion

  1. Reeves argued a fifteen-second spot is no different in kind from FDR's "fear itself." Was he right? What's lost when the soundbite comes without the speech?
  2. Stevenson said, "But I don't have to win." Is a candidate who refuses to simplify principled — or self-indulgent?
  3. Eisenhower stayed silent on McCarthy; McCain confronted his own supporters; Romney denounced his party's front-runner. What do we owe a politician who rebukes his own side, and why did it work so poorly for McCain and Romney?
  4. Is "serious politics" a style, a set of values, or a class marker? Does the critics' charge of hucksterism hold, or is it the 1952 "egghead" condescension in new form?
  5. Nixon's Checkers speech went over the heads of both the press and his own running mate. Is today's direct-to-voter communication the same move, or something different?
  6. Joe Alsop hoped the responsibility of power would sober the Republicans. Does winning moderate a movement — or embolden it?
  7. The "egghead" divide of 1952 split voters by education. How does that compare with today's educational divide between the parties?

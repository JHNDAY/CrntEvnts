What each speaker could count on

Every allusion is a bet about the audience. The speaker supplies a fragment and trusts listeners to supply the rest. How much each man leaves unsaid tells you what he assumed his listeners carried with them.

Lincoln · Second Inaugural

A nation that read one book

The speaker
Raised on the frontier with the King James Bible as one of the few books in the house. Never joined a church, but knew scripture as deeply as any preacher and read it as literature and argument.
The audience
Overwhelmingly Protestant, schooled on the same translation in homes, churches and McGuffey readers. A half-line of Matthew would summon the whole verse.
The occasion
A quiet, formal crowd, then a far larger audience reading the text in newspapers. Lincoln could write for the eye as well as the ear.
The bet
That listeners would recognize scripture without being told it was scripture.
Talarico · Election-night speech

A state that knows the stories

The speaker
A former middle-school language arts teacher who studied at Austin Presbyterian Theological Seminary, the grandson of a Baptist preacher and son of a preacher's daughter.
The audience
A Texas electorate in which faith is widespread but biblical literacy is uneven. Many know David and Goliath and the Good Samaritan; fewer could place a verse.
The occasion
A loud rally, with applause lines and live television and clips spreading online. The speech had to work by ear, in pieces.
The bet
That listeners would follow a story once it was named and told, and would accept a teacher's explanation of what it means.

The allusion ledger

Lincoln, 1865

Quoted and left for the listener

Shared Bible
Both read the same Bible and pray to the same God

Names the common text outright, then trusts the audience to know it as well as he does.

Genesis 3:19
wringing their bread from the sweat of other men's faces

Only someone who knew Eden's curse by heart would catch the inversion. Lincoln assumes they will.

Matthew 7:1
but let us judge not that we be not judged

Dropped into the sentence without quotation or credit, the way one uses a proverb everyone knows.

Matthew 18:7
Woe unto the world because of offences!…

Quoted whole with no citation. Listeners who knew the chapter would also recall the verses just before it, about harm done to "little ones."

Psalm 19:9
as was said three thousand years ago, so still it must be said

The only signal is a date. He trusts the crowd to know it is David's psalm.

1 Cor 13 (KJV)
With malice toward none, with charity for all

"Charity" in its King James sense, recognized by a churchgoing ear without comment.

Ps 147:3 · Lk 10:34 · Jas 1:27
to bind up the nation's wounds… his widow, and his orphan

Three passages layered into one plain sentence. No single source is needed to feel it.

Talarico, 2026

Retold and translated

1 Samuel 17
I'm reminded of that story from scripture of a shepherd boy who goes up against a giant.

He names the source and narrates it, with accurate details (forty days of taunts, the shield-bearer) that reward those who know it without losing those who don't.

Mt 22:37–40
he told me that we follow a barefoot rabbi who gave us two commandments

Jesus is never named. The phrase "barefoot rabbi" makes a familiar figure fresh and sounds like a grandfather's voice, not a pulpit's.

1 John 4:20
Because there is no love of God without love of neighbor.

The epistle's argument boiled down to a sentence a seventh-grader could repeat.

Genesis 1:27
Every single person bears the image of the sacred.

Doctrine in plain language. "The sacred" in place of "God" lets secular listeners stay with him.

1 Cor 13:4–7
Love is not passive, love is not neutral, love is not weak.

Borrows Paul's rhythm, which churchgoers hear at weddings, while the content stays his own.

Luke 2:7
she begged the manager to let us stay… I slept in a crib in her closet.

His own life told in the shape of the nativity. Those who catch it get a second layer; those who don't still get a moving story.

Mt 21:12 · Jn 2:15
Two thousand years ago… he flipped over the tables

Signaled by a date, as Lincoln does with Psalm 19, then retold so no one is left behind.

Two storytellers

Both men reach their audiences through story, and both fold their stories into the Bible's. They tell different kinds of story, though. In the Second Inaugural, Lincoln tells one story, the nation's, in a few hundred words. Talarico tells half a dozen, most of them from his own life.

Lincoln was famous as a storyteller in conversation, on the stump and in his debates with Douglas, where a homely anecdote could carry an argument. At the Capitol in 1865 he set the anecdotes aside. What remains is narrative craft: a plot, a turning point, irony, and a cast of characters.

Lincoln

The nation as one story

Opening
"On the occasion corresponding to this four years ago…" He begins as a narrator looking back.
Turning point
"Both parties deprecated war; but one of them would make war rather than let the nation survive; and the other would accept war rather than let it perish. And the war came." Four short words move the whole plot forward.
Irony
"Neither party expected for the war, the magnitude, or the duration, which it has already attained… Each looked for an easier triumph." The characters misjudge their own story.
Author
"The Almighty has His own purposes." The plot belongs to God, and Matthew 18:7 supplies its moral.
Cast
"him who shall have borne the battle, and … his widow, and his orphan." Unnamed figures every listener could picture, because most knew one.
Talarico

Many stories, one pattern

Scripture
David and Goliath, retold with its details and then turned on the room: "You were my slingshot."
Classroom
His students on San Antonio's west side: "survivors, dreamers, fighters."
Family
His mother's escape, the hotel manager, the crib in the closet. The nativity shape is never stated.
Lineage
His grandfather the Baptist preacher, who hands down the "barefoot rabbi" and the two commandments.
Campaign
Volunteers carrying water to Sand Branch and school supplies to El Sauz, small parables of love of neighbor.
Closing
Jesus in the Temple, told as a scene: "He walked into the seat of power…"

The two men make the same move in opposite directions. Lincoln places his audience inside a biblical story. Americans become the world of Matthew 18, living through the woe that follows an offence, so the listener becomes a character in scripture. Talarico places biblical stories inside his audience's lives. David is the campaign volunteer, and the room with no space for a child is his mother's apartment in East Austin, so scripture becomes a character in the listener's world.

Talarico also brings his stories back and lets them pay off. "Love can turn a closet into a nursery" recalls his mother's story, and "every David against every Goliath" recalls his opening. Callbacks like these help a live crowd follow a long speech. Lincoln's speech is short enough to need none.

Where they started

Both men came from modest beginnings, and Lincoln's were the harder of the two. Yet Lincoln kept his origins out of his speeches, while Talarico makes his the vehicle for his message. Temperament explains part of the difference. The way speeches reached the public in each era explains much of the rest.

Lincoln

"The short and simple annals"

Origins
Born in a Kentucky log cabin in 1809 and raised on the Indiana frontier. His mother died when he was nine. All told, less than a year of formal schooling.
In 1860
When John Locke Scripps came to write his campaign biography, Lincoln told him there was little to make of his early life. He supplied a brief autobiographical sketch, written in the third person.
The image
Built by others. At the Illinois Republican convention in May 1860, his cousin John Hanks carried in fence rails said to be split by Lincoln, and "the Rail Splitter" was born.
In the speech
Not a word about his own life. His suffering appears only as the nation's.
Talarico

The crib in the closet

Origins
Born to a young mother who fled an abusive partner and found shelter, first in the hotel where she worked, then in a small apartment in East Austin.
Path
Then a more conventional climb than Lincoln's: university, a public school classroom on San Antonio's west side, graduate study and seminary.
The image
Built by himself. He tells his own story, in his own voice, as the core of his message.
In the speech
Told at length and in biblical shapes: the underdog, the shepherd boy, the child with no room.
Lincoln to Scripps, 1860, as recalled by Scripps
It is a great piece of folly to attempt to make anything out of my early life. It can all be condensed into a single sentence… "The short and simple annals of the poor."
⟷
Talarico, 2026
There wasn't room for a nursery, so I slept in a crib in her closet. And she was so proud of that closet.

Lincoln borrows a line from Thomas Gray's "Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard" to close the subject. Talarico opens it and lingers on the detail. One treats poverty as private; the other treats it as testimony.

How the medium shaped the story. A speech in 1865 was heard by the crowd in front of the Capitol, but most Americans met it days later as text in a newspaper, read and reread. Talarico's speech reached the room, live television and phones across the country at the same moment, and short clips of it were circulating within minutes.

The medium does not explain everything. Lincoln was reserved about his past even in private, and many modern politicians manage to say little of their own lives. But the shift from print to screen helps explain why the same biblical habit of mind produced such different speeches. In Lincoln's world a man's poverty was something to rise above quietly. In Talarico's it is a story to share, and the Bible supplies its shape.

How well does each connect?

Lincoln

Recognition

Why it worked
  • Unexplained quotation flatters the listener: "you know this as well as I do." That shared knowledge makes speaker and audience equals.
  • His prose and the King James text are so close in style that scripture seems to rise naturally out of the argument.
  • By claiming no special religious standing, he left no one to resent a lecture.
Limits
  • The method depends entirely on a shared text. Today many of his allusions pass unnoticed, and the speech has to be annotated to be fully heard.
  • Recognition does not guarantee agreement. Lincoln himself judged the speech "not immediately popular."
I expect the latter to wear as well as—perhaps better than—any thing I have produced; but I believe it is not immediately popular.Lincoln to Thurlow Weed, March 15, 1865
Talarico

Translation

Why it works
  • Naming and retelling each story includes listeners whose biblical knowledge is thin, while accurate details still reward those who know the text well.
  • His teacher's habit of explaining, then giving a memorable line to take home ("always bet on the underdog"), fits a mixed crowd.
  • Telling his own life in scriptural shapes lets listeners recognize the Bible in an ordinary Texas family.
  • Moving between church cadence and current slang signals that he belongs in both worlds.
Limits
  • Explaining scripture puts him in the preacher's role, which some listeners welcome and others resist.
  • Citing his family and faith as authority invites listeners to judge his theology. Some conservative Christian critics have disputed it, so the connection is strong with some believers and weak with others.
  • Secular listeners may hear religious framing in a political speech as exclusion, however plain the language.

Two kinds of believer

The two speakers stand in very different places as men of faith. Talarico speaks from inside the church. Lincoln spoke from its doorway, as a lifelong reader of the Bible who never joined a congregation. Each man's relationship to faith shapes how he uses scripture.

Lincoln

The reader who never joined

Practice
Never joined a church or made a public profession of faith. In Washington he rented a pew at New York Avenue Presbyterian and attended often.
Early doubt
As a young man in New Salem he was known for skeptical talk about religion, a reputation that followed him for years.
Attacked
In his 1846 race for Congress, his opponent, the Methodist circuit rider Peter Cartwright, was accused of spreading the charge that Lincoln was an "infidel." Lincoln answered in a printed handbill.
Deepening
The deaths of his sons Eddie (1850) and Willie (1862), and the weight of the war, pushed him toward a sober belief in Providence.
The Bible
Read it closely all his life. Thanking a delegation of Black Baltimoreans for a gift Bible in 1864, he called it "the best gift God has given to man."
Talarico

The seminarian in politics

Practice
A Christian who speaks openly about his faith, raised in a family of Baptist preachers and teachers.
Training
Studied at Austin Presbyterian Theological Seminary while serving in the Texas House. His theology is formal as well as inherited.
Attacked
Critics on the religious right have called his theology unorthodox or worse, especially on questions such as abortion where his views depart from theirs.
Stance
Presents faith as the source of his politics, and challenges what he sees as Christian nationalism from within Christianity.
The Bible
Read as a teacher reads it: stories to explain, apply and pass on.
Lincoln, 1846 handbill
That I am not a member of any Christian Church, is true; but I have never denied the truth of the Scriptures.
⟷
Talarico, 2026
My faith teaches me that love is the strongest force in the universe.

Both men had their faith questioned by opponents, but from opposite sides. Lincoln was charged with having too little religion; Talarico is charged with having the wrong kind. Lincoln's answer is a careful legal defense. Talarico's is open testimony.

How it shows in the speeches. Lincoln's uncertainty becomes the theology of the Second Inaugural. A private note he wrote around 1862, later titled "Meditation on the Divine Will," reads like a first draft: "In great contests each party claims to act in accordance with the will of God. Both may be, and one must be wrong." In 1865 he does not say what God wants. He says "If God wills," "as God gives us to see the right," and "The Almighty has His own purposes." His doubt makes him humble about God's purposes, never about scripture itself.

Talarico's confidence works the other way. He says "Scripture has taught me" and "My faith teaches me," and he tells the audience what the stories mean. He speaks with a believer's assurance, and his authority is personal and visible in a way Lincoln's never was.

This also explains the difference in technique traced above. A man without a church could not claim to speak for one, so Lincoln let the text speak for itself and stood beside his listeners as a fellow reader. A man formed by the church can speak as its witness, so Talarico testifies.

I am profitably engaged in reading the Bible. Take all of this book upon reason that you can, and the balance on faith, and you will, I am confident, live and die a better man.Lincoln to Joshua Speed, as recalled by Speed, 1864

The bottom line

Both men speak the Bible as a native tongue, and both use it to make a political audience feel addressed as a people. What changed is the listener.

Lincoln could assume a shared text, so he connected through recognition. He quoted without explaining, and his audience met him halfway. Talarico cannot assume the same knowledge, so he connects through translation. He names the story, tells it, explains it, and ties it to his own life, which is how a good teacher reaches a room of mixed preparation.

The Lincoln you hear in Talarico is real, and it shows most in the paired cadences, the dating of scripture instead of citing it, and the plain words "enemy" and "friend." Where Talarico departs from Lincoln is in putting himself and his family in the frame, and in that he is much closer to the Black church tradition and Martin Luther King Jr., who also bridged pulpit and politics by narrating scripture through personal testimony.

The storytelling points to one more echo. Talarico's homely, self-deprecating anecdotes ("teaching middle school is the best preparation for politics") recall the Lincoln of the stump and the parlor, the teller of frontier stories, more than the Lincoln of the Second Inaugural. What the two share most is the conviction that a story reaches people an argument cannot.

Their origins show the same divide. Lincoln, whose start was the humbler of the two, waved his away as "the short and simple annals of the poor" and left the legend to others. Talarico makes his own early life the vehicle for his message. Part of that is temperament, and part is the medium: a printed speech read over days rewards restraint, while a speech carried instantly in clips rewards a vivid personal story.

Finally, faith. Talarico speaks as a believer and Lincoln as a respectful, searching reader, yet both had their faith attacked by opponents, and both knew the Bible well enough to make it speak to people who did not share their politics. The Bible moved Lincoln as a text even where his belief stayed uncertain. For Talarico it is both a text and a faith he professes. That difference may be the deepest reason one speech asks questions of God and the other gives answers.

Questions for discussion

  1. Could a politician today quote "Woe unto the world because of offences" without explanation and be understood? What would be lost by explaining it?
  2. Lincoln speaks as a fellow reader of scripture; Talarico speaks as heir to a family of preachers. Which stance makes you more willing to listen, and why?
  3. Talarico tells his mother's story in the shape of the nativity without naming it. Is an unspoken allusion more powerful than a stated one?
  4. Lincoln tells the nation's story and leaves himself out; Talarico tells his own. Which kind of story makes a scriptural point land harder for you?
  5. If Lincoln had campaigned in the age of video and social media, would he have told his own story? Would voters today accept a candidate who said his early life was not worth discussing?
  6. Does a speaker need to be a believer to use scripture with integrity? Does Lincoln's uncertainty make his use of the Bible more persuasive or less?
  7. Is there a common text today that could do for a national audience what the King James Bible did in 1865?

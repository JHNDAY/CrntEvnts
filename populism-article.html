Democrats Need to Embrace Big-Tent Populism

By Daron Acemoglu
Read full article →
Core Argument

Populism can mobilize power and govern effectively—but only if it unites rather than divides. "Big-tent populism" must appeal to both workers and the middle classes, businesses and progressives.

Why populism matters

Workers left behind by growth. Affordability crisis real. Non-college-educated Americans haven't benefited from half-century of gains.

The risk

Purely adversarial populism frames everything as oppressor vs. oppressed. This alienates the middle class and prevents broad coalition-building.

Historical models of broad coalitions

1930s Sweden
SAP abandoned Marxism, appealed to middle classes and farmers alongside workers. Built bridges with corporations. Result: postwar high-wage economy.
1930s–40s America
FDR's New Deal: brain trust, labor movement, conservative Southern Democrats. Strange bedfellows united by shared prosperity agenda.
Contemporary danger signals

NYC Mayor Zohran Mamdani: "I don't think we should have billionaires"

Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez: "You can't earn a billion dollars"

Acemoglu's concern: can a politics built on blame—even justified blame—actually build and govern a majority?

What centrists are right about

Governance requires feasible policy, not slogans. Delivering services to citizens matters.

What progressives are right about

Need new ideas to tackle inequality, create opportunity, limit corporate excess. Status quo doesn't work.

The synthesis: a governing philosophy that speaks for ordinary people in their language—not by seeking oppressors, real or imaginary, but by offering shared prosperity.

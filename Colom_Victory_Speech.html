"Mississippi Matters"

Scott Colom's Victory Speech — Five Themes in Sixteen Minutes

Scott Colom opens and closes his speech the same way: "God bless you." In between, he builds a short sermon on three words: prayer, faith and work. He uses them to frame the campaign, the history of his state, and the promises he makes to voters.

Victory speeches usually celebrate the winner. This one spends most of its time naming other people: civil rights figures, the ordinary Mississippians he met on the campaign trail, mentors, staff and family. The five themes below show how he does it. Click a heading to explore each one.

Theme One

"It's Something That We Did"

Colom's humility: giving away the credit for his own win

Colom keeps shifting the credit away from himself. He talks about predicting victory "for months and months," then immediately says people will give him too much credit. His own part, he says, was only "the courage to imagine a night like this."

He is also frank about the lean times. Donors wouldn't call back, and fewer than ten people came to his first town hall in Jackson. He admits he was tired and doubted he could go on. Telling those stories on victory night makes the win belong to the people who stuck with him.

He even tells a story at his own expense. His mentors told him he wasn't campaigning enough. He took it as a challenge from a competitive twin, and he calls the victory "a testament to" Congressman Thompson's strategy rather than his own.

Theme Two

"The Ones That Didn't Vote for Me"

Colom reaching out to the audience and to every Mississippian

Colom talks with the crowd, not at it. He says "y'all" again and again, and he calls on the room to applaud the civil rights veterans in the audience. He looks for his finance director in the crowd ("Where's Amanda at?"). When someone shouts "You did!" he answers, "And I did."

The policy content of the speech comes through three people he met on the campaign trail, not through a platform. A soybean farmer in Oktibbeha County lost a customer to tariffs. A single mother of three in George County drives to Alabama for work and is squeezed by gas prices. A kidney transplant survivor in Hattiesburg worries about Medicaid cuts. All three share one trait: "But she continued to work."

His closing reaches beyond his supporters. He addresses those who voted for him, those who didn't, and those who didn't vote at all. His promises are personal ones, built on the Golden Rule, rather than partisan ones.

Theme Three

Prayer · Faith · Work

The religious framing that structures the whole speech

The speech is built like a three-part sermon. Prayer begins the journey. Faith carries him through doubt. Work completes it, introduced through his mother's quotation of the Epistle of James: "faith without works is dead."

He even gives the win a religious meaning: "God didn't want us to get it the easy way. He wanted us to get it the Mississippi way." Hardship becomes part of a plan, not just bad luck.

The final promise brings all three words back together. He commits to staying in prayer, keeping faith and continuing to work. The language comes from the Black church tradition and from evangelical Mississippi more broadly. That lets one vocabulary speak to audiences that are often split politically.

Colom places himself in a line of succession. He starts with Hiram Revels, a Mississippi minister who became the nation's first Black senator, and the connection is plain: a man of prayer from Mississippi, going to the U.S. Senate.

Each figure is presented as an example of faith in Mississippi itself. Evers believed the state could change. Hamer kept coming home. He uses that pattern to answer a common view of the state, that progress there is impossible.

He then broadens the tribute from famous names to "local people" who did the work "without any expectation of recognition." That links the movement's heroes to the volunteers in the room.

Theme Five

"I've Already Won the Most Important One"

Colom's tribute to his family

The family section is the warmest part of the speech. His wife, Nadia, gets the longest single tribute. He thanks her not only as a spouse who raised their two daughters while he was on the road, but also as part of the campaign: "She managed the food truck."

He credits the wider family network, the grandmothers Brenda Dale and Dorothy Colom, who helped with the children. He praises his daughters, Lucy and Maddie, for holding up "under tough circumstances." Then he thanks parents, his brother, sister-in-law, aunts and cousins.

His mother appears twice more. Her advice that "faith without works is dead" is the turning point of the speech. Her suggestion to a student named Gerard Henderson led, eventually, to his becoming Colom's campaign manager. His identical twin brother explains his competitive streak, and his brother also made a campaign ad.

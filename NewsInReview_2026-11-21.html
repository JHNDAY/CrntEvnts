The Three Musketeers: A Trilateral Summit

Before moving on to a wrap-up of the midterms, let's discuss this week's trilateral summit among the U.S., China and Russia—the first such meeting ever held among the three countries. For me, it evoked the title of the famous Alexandre Dumas novel. Trump would clearly have to be the portly musketeer, Porthos, and to take the analogy farther, Trump had mused about Kim Jong Un also attending; he could then have played the role of a considerably less dashing and appealing D'Artagnan. All four men undoubtedly would have found Cardinal Richelieu the character to admire.

Each of the principals had different reasons for being there:

Trump: It was an opportunity to get away from the aftermath of the midterms and meet with authoritarian "friends," whom he admires and would like to emulate. In his mind, it was a chance to leverage his stellar interpersonal and negotiating skills. In this regard, the Washington Post commented that "Trump's ambitions may exceed his leverage." Undoubtedly he was comfortable with the 81-minute length of the meeting, which would have accommodated a two-hour TV show with ample commercials.

Xi: Hosting the meeting and seated at the middle of the table, Xi had an opportunity to elevate his personal standing and position China as equal to the U.S. To soften any potentially steadfast U.S. positions, he played to Trump's vanity by breaking precedent and meeting him at the airport.

Putin: For an international pariah engaged in a prolonged invasion of Ukraine—where his forces have brutalized civilians and suffered an estimated 1.4 million casualties of their own—former ambassador to Russia Michael McFaul characterized the summit as "a photo-op for Putin to help him push the myth that Russia is a respected great power." This is underscored by Russia's 2026 nominal GDP of only $2.9T, compared to $32.6T for the U.S. and $21.6T for China. Russia has been characterized as a gas station with nukes.

Going into the meeting, CNN's Nick Paton Walsh asked: "Three strongman leaders over age 70, 90% of the world's nuclear weapons, two ongoing wars, one room, and no specific agenda. What could possibly go wrong?"

Light on Results, Long on Symbolism

Nothing seriously went wrong because, as Andrea Mitchell wrote, the meeting was "light on results, but long on symbolism." Here are a few takeaways:

  • Going into the meeting, Trump told the press that he was meeting with the two leaders because "They both like Trump."
  • At the meeting he told Xi, "I think as long as you and I are in charge of things, we'll never have a problem."

The joint statement issued after the meeting said that the three countries:

  • "committed to reform the UN so that the UN can respond more effectively to global challenges"
  • "plan to increase the production and supply of refined petroleum products for the benefit of all nations"
  • "concurred that the International Criminal Court and other similar bodies must not abuse their jurisdiction"
  • "reaffirmed that a nuclear war cannot be won and must never be fought"

The statement did not address China and Russia's support of Iran as it battles the U.S.

Apparently the Ukraine war was discussed, as this was an opportunity to exclude Ukraine from the process. It is likely that Trump's support of Russia's talking points may increase in the aftermath.

"You cannot make decisions about any country, even a small one, without that country's participation." Volodymyr Zelensky, on being excluded from the talks

Although the parties agreed to "conduct a dialogue on reducing nuclear risks," there likely was nothing concrete, because China is committed to reducing its nuclear weapons gap. Trump separately stated that "we oppose any form of global regulation" of AI. In the absence of any significant agreements, Simone McCarthy of CNN opined, "The talks themselves are the deliverable."

Midterms Wrap-up: The House

With all but a handful of races called, here is a summary of the results. Some of this material repeats last week's, but I wanted this newsletter to stand on its own.

CNN's current tally is 258 D vs. 172 R seats. Three races in MN and NC are undergoing recounts, with Democratic leads of 300+ votes in two of them and a Republican lead of 2,500 votes in the other. It is very likely that the leaders will prevail in all three races. The two remaining uncalled races are in Louisiana, where runoffs are scheduled for December 12. Because the combined Republican vote exceeded that for the Democrats by 15–20% in each race, Republicans are very likely to win both runoffs, one of which is in a newly gerrymandered district.

This brings the tally to 260 D vs. 175 R, a 45-seat net flip to the Democrats, who flipped 49 seats vs. only 4 for the Republicans—3 of which came from the 15 Trump-initiated gerrymanders. The 5 California retaliatory gerrymanders and the non-partisan one in Utah all succeeded for the Democrats; this means that the Democrats picked up a net of 3 seats from redistricting. That said, had the election not been a Blue wave, the Republicans likely would have fared considerably better in this regard.

The 260 Democratic seats, only 8 of which are held by DSA members, fall short of the 267 seats they held after the 1990 midterms and barely exceed the 257 seats they held after the 2008 election.

Although the generic House ballot forecast a 9% advantage for the Democrats, the actual margin is 14%, the largest since the post-Watergate midterms 52 years ago. The main reason the polls were so far off appears to be that Democratic turnout was unexpectedly high, especially among young voters and Black voters, whereas Republican turnout was generally lower, particularly among evangelicals.

Not to get greedy, but the results could have been even better for the Democrats: among the 37 races determined by a margin of 3% or less, the Republicans won 26 vs. 11 for the Democrats.

The Senate: Nine Flips and an Enduring Advantage

Democrat Mary Peltola was declared the winner in Alaska this week as her vote total reached 50.1% vs. 45.8% for incumbent Daniel S. Sullivan, while his ballyhooed namesake Daniel J. Sullivan got only 1.7% of the vote.

The Democrats flipped 9 of the 22 Republican Senate seats and successfully defended all 13 of theirs, reversing the count to 22 D vs. 13 R. This 9-seat gain was the greatest since 1994, when the Republicans flipped a net of 10 seats. Because senators hold their seats for 6 years, special elections aside, this gives the Democrats an enduring advantage in the Senate, especially with 18 R seats vs. 16 D seats up for election in 2028.

In the 9 states Trump won by 20% or more in 2024, the Democrats won 5 Senate races (KS, MS, NC, SC and TX), and in the other 4 such races, they gained at least 20% in losing efforts. The only Democratic Senate candidate who fared worse than Kamala Harris as a percentage of the vote was Troy Jackson in Maine, who was 1% lower, as he faced the popular Susan Collins.

Contrary to the House races, where the Republicans won 26 of the 37 closest races, the Democrats won 6 of the 8 Senate races determined by 5% or less:

Progressives and the Blue Wave

Flashing her progressive credentials, AOC commented, "If Abdul can win in Michigan, anybody can win anywhere." However, this is not borne out by a careful look at the election data. Nate Cohn wrote that "The party's weakest results tended to come when the Democrats nominated progressive candidates in high profile primaries."

Moreover, the senatorial candidates who performed the worst against the Blue wave—measured against their states' party preference in the last two presidential elections—were, in decreasing order: Troy Jackson in Maine, Peggy Flanagan in Minnesota, Angie Nixon in Florida, and Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, all of whom are staunch progressives.

In the context of such data, Jess Bidgood praised Pete Buttigieg, saying, "What a party dazzled by progressive forces really needs is a moderate red-state whisperer like himself."

Governor and State Legislative Races

Governors: Going into the midterms, the Republicans held a 26–24 advantage in governor's chairs, but the Democrats flipped 6 chairs—in AK, GA, IA, NV, OH and TX—giving them a 30–20 advantage, including all 7 presidential battlegrounds and encompassing 75% of the U.S. population.

Trifectas: Going into the midterms, the Republicans held 23 trifectas, the Democrats 16, and 11 states had divided governance. As a result of the gubernatorial and state legislative races, the Democrats gained 6 trifectas in AZ, MI, MN, NV, PA and WI (5 are battlegrounds) and broke Republican trifectas in GA, IA, NH, OH and TX (2 are battlegrounds). The new trifecta tally is 22 D, 18 R and 10 divided.

Legislatures: Going into the midterms, the Republicans controlled 57 chambers to 39 for the Democrats, with 2 shared. The Democrats flipped 12 chambers with no Republican flips, changing the tally to 51 D, 45 R and 2 shared. Whereas the Republicans currently hold 55.5% of all state legislative seats to 44.5% for the Democrats, next year the Democrats will hold 52.7% vs. 47.3%. This will be the first time since 2010 that the Democrats control the majority of state chambers.

I'd Be Remiss Not to Mention

Anthropic's IPO, which valued the company at $1.5T, closed its first day ranked 7th globally with about $1.8T in capitalization. It instantly created about 5,100 employee millionaires, with 400 early hires each worth at least $100 million.

Nvidia: In the wake of its Q3 financial report, Nvidia's market capitalization exceeded $3.5T, making it the third most valuable company in the world—behind Apple and barely behind Microsoft—before its value plummeted by $900B later in the week.

At home, the final tallies confirm the scale of the Blue wave: a 45-seat House gain, the largest Senate swing since 1994, a 30–20 governors' advantage and the first Democratic majority of state chambers since 2010. Yet the close-race data and the underperformance of progressive nominees suggest the wave was broad rather than ideological—a lesson both parties will be weighing as 2028 comes into view.

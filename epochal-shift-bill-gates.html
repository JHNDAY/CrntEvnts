An Epochal Shift

The turbulent AI era is here. The choices we make now are critical. We need a plan to ensure that the good outweighs the bad.

Foundation

What You Need to Know

  • AI represents one of the most transformative technologies in human history
  • We remain dangerously unprepared for its arrival
  • AI could become the greatest equalizer or worst source of injustice
  • We vastly underestimate AI's impact because it still makes mistakes
  • AI operates at unprecedented speed and can replicate human cognition
  • The outcome hinges entirely on choices we make now

The transition to the AI era will be one of the most turbulent times in human history. Right now, we are not preparing adequately for that transition. If the world takes the right steps, AI will be a force for good and leave everyone better off.

During my entire life I've only had two jobs. In the first one, I played a role in developing software to empower people through my work at Microsoft. In my second one, which I started full time in 2008, I am giving back the wealth I made at Microsoft with the goal of making the world a healthier, better educated, and more equitable place.

Both of these experiences inform my perspective on artificial intelligence. When I first learned about computers at age 13 I was fascinated by the idea of making them more intelligent and able to perform things that, at the time, only humans could do. Although the term "AI" was used from around the time I was born, the technology has only made significant progress in the last decade. It is now incredibly capable and it is continuing to improve at a mind-blowing rate. AI for the first time can replace and even exceed human cognition.

AI will either be the greatest equalizer ever invented, or the worst source of injustice.

In terms of equity, AI will either be the greatest equalizer ever invented, or the worst source of injustice. The challenge is monumental. How will we use this technology to make the world a fairer place and keep it from widening the divide between rich and poor?

Why We're Underestimating AI

Many commentators underestimate the extent of the impact AI will have. One reason is that AI models still make mistakes. But the reliability problem is being fixed quickly, as researchers create models that can check their own work and improve themselves.

Another reason people underestimate AI is that analogies to past innovations are misleading. We have no experience with a technology that can be adopted quickly or that can think and move like a human. When the PC came along, it took twenty years to significantly change how we worked. AI, on the other hand, runs on the devices we already have, and it uses natural language. We don't have to adapt to it because it can adapt to us.

Why This Time Really Is Different

For as long as I can remember, I've wished innovation could happen faster. With AI, my feelings are more complicated. I wish the world could get the benefits rapidly and delay the problems, but the benefits and problems are arriving at the same time.

Many observers say this transition will be like previous ones. They give the example of how jobs shifted from agriculture to office work. However, that proceeded over several generations and created new jobs where human cognition was required. In this case, the technology can substitute for human cognition. AI will hit industries rapidly, over the course of a decade rather than a few generations.

If someone had a credible plan for slowing down AI advances globally, I would likely support it. However, I don't think that's going to happen. The geopolitical and economic incentives are pushing too hard to go full speed ahead.

The Challenge

Three Big Risks

  • Job displacement will accelerate across all sectors faster than any previous technological transition
  • Disruption will occur within a single decade rather than generations
  • AI will empower harmful actors while concentrating existing power structures
  • Enables cyberattacks, bioterrorism, and manipulation at unprecedented scales
  • AI companions risk undermining human development, particularly in children
  • Children will lose resilience built through real relationships and productive struggle
  • These three risks compound each other, creating cascading economic and security consequences

Risk 1: Many Jobs Will Disappear Forever

The jobs at most risk are entry- and mid-level, and the new jobs being created will mostly require skills that take many years to learn. White-collar jobs are already being hit modestly. After widespread adoption of generative AI, employment fell significantly among young workers in vulnerable roles.

I think this trend will continue across many industries. Jobs in sales, customer support, software engineering, and paralegal work may be among the first affected. But disruption will reach much further—assessing loan applications, data analysis, even triaging patients.

Blue-collar jobs will be affected as well. "Smart" robots will begin to compete with people on physical tasks in construction and hospitality by the end of the decade. Robots and AI combined can create a vicious cycle of automation acceleration.

I'm especially worried about young people entering a workforce with fewer entry-level openings. The biggest shift will happen when AI provides nearly error-free work and can function without human oversight. This will lead to a fundamental change in how we think about work, income, and economic security.

In a capitalist society, employment is the way most people get money for basics and a key source of dignity and social connection. When communities have high unemployment, the ripple effects are pervasive. Research suggests factory closures contribute to opioid overdoses. Now imagine similar pressures nationwide.

We have to think now about how to reduce job losses so that everyone can share in the prosperity that AI creates.

Risk 2: AI Will Empower People to Do More Harm

AI will make it much easier to create weapons and target victims at scale. Even criminals with very limited skills will be able to target individuals, companies, and governments. AI-enabled fraud, disinformation, deepfakes, and surveillance are harms people will feel keenly in everyday life.

The smartest cybersecurity experts I know are scared about the next few years. Attackers are getting powerful new capabilities faster than defenders can fix weaknesses. The same AI model that finds software flaws can also help criminals exploit them.

Think about vulnerable infrastructure: hospitals, financial institutions, water systems, power grids, government benefits systems. When these are attacked, patients, customers, and recipients stand to lose.

Bioterrorism is another concern. Although AI will lead to lifesaving advances in drugs and vaccines, it will also make it easier to design deadly diseases. The positive capabilities are hard to separate from dangerous ones.

The same tools will also concentrate power where it already exists. Autonomous weapons will make governments more capable of using deadly force without human involvement. Monitoring and manipulating public opinion will be easier, cheaper, and more effective.

AI models could begin to act against our interests and we could lose control.

Risk 3: AI Could Stunt Children's Development

I doubt I would have put in the work to develop social skills if I had an AI companion growing up. They talk in ways you're comfortable with, never push you outside your comfort zone, and never get mad. This gives them potential to become highly addictive and rob us of lessons from human connection.

Research shows that people with smaller social networks are most likely to turn to chatbots for companionship, and heavier use correlates with feeling worse. Young people could be affected for their entire lives.

Countries including Australia, UK, and Norway are adopting protections for children online. China has restricted AI companion apps, barred designs fostering dependence, and banned virtual relatives/romantic partners for minors.

Ironically, the same tool that will allow people to learn more could also lead to learning less. One survey suggested heavier AI use correlated with less critical thinking, especially among younger people. This is the worst time to lose critical thinking skills when deepfakes and tailored misinformation are prevalent.

AI may help in some cases—for isolated elderly people or those with limited mobility. Wherever we draw the line on these problems, it should be our decision, made intentionally.

The Opportunity

The Good Things We Can Do With AI

  • AI will accelerate scientific research exponentially across all fields
  • Enables breakthrough discoveries in clean energy, climate, food production, and disease eradication
  • Healthcare transforms—rural hospitals gain specialist capabilities, doctors make better diagnoses
  • Agriculture revolutionizes for low-income farmers who access knowledge previously available only to the wealthy
  • Government services become accessible rather than bureaucratic nightmares
  • Education personalizes at scale; mental health support reaches underserved communities
  • These benefits won't flow automatically—require deliberate policy and investment to reach beyond wealthy nations

Some people see only the upside of AI; others focus exclusively on dangers. We need both: deep concern about harms and grounded optimism about benefits maximized for everyone.

Maximizing benefits is just as important as minimizing harms. If people see how AI makes their lives easier, it builds public trust. If AI's first impact is taking away jobs, skeptics will reject it entirely, making it harder to deliver benefits.

Innovation & Scientific Research

With its ability to synthesize knowledge from every scientific field, AI can accelerate innovation in critical challenges: clean energy, climate change, food production, disease eradication. Researchers can use AI to search through massive scientific literature, identify patterns humans might miss, and determine promising experiments.

When intelligence is no longer the limiting factor, smaller companies can compete with large research budgets. R&D and innovation will be supercharged.

Healthcare

Many small American hospitals lack on-site specialists for life-threatening emergencies. AI could ensure heart attacks are caught in time and families avoid crushing medical expenses. Viz.ai analyzes scans to detect strokes and emergencies in nearly 2,000 U.S. hospitals.

AI will help primary-care doctors make better diagnoses and keep in touch with patients outside the clinic. It will help patients understand test results and medicine schedules.

Agriculture in Low-Income Countries

Agriculture is where I see the fastest AI impact in low-income countries. Most farmers don't get reliable weather forecasts or advice on seeds, crop protection, or soil improvement. With population growth and climate challenges, they need more help than ever. Using AI, farmers will get better advice than even the richest farmers today and increase output substantially.

Government Services

I've met families overwhelmed by applying for health insurance, student aid, or food assistance. Faced with huge bureaucratic forms, many felt like giving up. AI can streamline dramatically—citizens get help faster, government operates more efficiently. This especially benefits those needing safety net services most.

Mental Health Support

Most communities have too few counselors, psychiatrists, and addiction specialists. With proper privacy safeguards, AI tools could help recognize warning signs, offer evidence-based coping strategies, and team up with humans for responsive treatment.

Education

AI can free teachers to spend more time one-on-one with students and show where whole classes struggle. AI tools preserving "productive struggle"—the cognitive work that builds understanding—strengthen learning. When encountering new ideas, AI gives explanations and questions. Later, when checking comprehension, it holds answers back, helping students arrive at them independently.

We have to be deliberate about ensuring that it benefits everyone and not just a wealthy few.

AI could give individuals and small businesses access to capabilities requiring expensive help or large staffs. It could help people with disabilities live independently and enable entrepreneurs to accomplish far more.

Most importantly, AI could give people back time and attention consumed by paperwork, bureaucracy, information searching, and unaffordable tasks. Multiplied across millions of lives, these benefits would be profound.

The operative word is "can"—AI can improve life at every income level. But it won't automatically. We must be deliberate about ensuring benefits reach all, requiring government and philanthropy to help less wealthy citizens and low-income countries.

The Response

A Comprehensive Plan Forward

  • Need unprecedented institutional response matching AI's scope and speed
  • Create new domestic and international governance frameworks coordinating across all affected sectors
  • Explicitly reserve certain jobs for humans for both economic and ethical reasons
  • Caregiving, delivering difficult news, and teaching should not be automated
  • Tax AI and robots like we tax labor to slow automation's economic incentives
  • Fund robust retraining programs and create stronger social safety nets
  • Ensure dignified work remains available and prevent wealth concentration

Solutions should be developed through public democratic process including elected officials, policymakers, educators, health workers, local and community leaders. Millions will be disrupted; we need a stronger, flexible social safety net.

Solutions should be shaped by answers to profound questions about preserving humanity when machines out-think us. Religious leaders can play a key role. Pope Leo XIV's encyclical "On Safeguarding the Human Person in the Time of Artificial Intelligence" lays a strong foundation.

Strategy 1: Build a New System for Managing the Transition

The highest priority is creating a domestic and international framework for AI. None of our current institutions were designed for technology spreading so fast and touching so many lives.

After 9/11, the U.S. underwent its biggest reorganization since WWII for just one function—national security. AI will require much more, affecting national security, employment, education, taxation, energy, elections, environment, public health, finance, law enforcement, transportation, lands, and IT systems.

These sectors overlap in ways bureaucracy isn't designed to manage. A labor department understands workforce disruption but not security risk. Business regulators understand market concentration but not AI's effects on children. Left alone, institutions see only one part while consequences ripple across the entire system.

At national level, countries need bodies setting priorities across agencies to ensure every risk is accounted for. An international organization must be built in parallel—unlike anything created before, but following elements from nuclear weapons inspections regimes, international aviation regulations, and ozone layer agreements.

Government moves slowly, and polarization makes cooperation harder than ever. Some U.S.-China cooperation will be required. We lack luxury of moving slowly. Leaders should convene economists, technologists, labor experts, business leaders, and workers to identify failing institutions and needed authorities. Countries must learn from each other.

Countries hosting leading AI developers and controlling critical supply chain parts should meet now to set shared norms before competitive pressure prevents cooperation. Building this framework will take years—start now.

Strategy 2: Set Aside Some Jobs for Humans

My dad died of Alzheimer's in 2020, cared for day and night by paid caregivers who understood him even when he struggled. No robot could or should have done that. Something in that care was irreplaceably human.

I believe as AI and robots improve, we'll set aside certain things for only people to do. I call this "Human Reserved"—like nature reserves where we could build but choose not to because loss would be too great.

We might set something aside for economic reasons—when allowing machines would displace people unable to easily change jobs. You can't tell a 55-year-old construction worker they should work in elder care and expect fulfillment.

Sometimes decisions will be driven by other factors. Imagine a robot giving awful news of incurable disease. No technical reason prevents it, yet it shouldn't. The Human Reserved domain will evolve—phase in AI slowly over years or decades while preserving some jobs. Education and mental health care will be mixed, with humans in charge using technology to extend capabilities.

Lines will vary by place. Some countries might insist on human elder care. Japan, with shrinking workforce and few young people caring for elderly, may welcome caregiving robots.

Human Reserved raises many unanswered questions: Who decides what we reserve? What criteria apply? How prevent companies from cheating? What happens to international trade? These must be worked out publicly as part of transition planning.

Strategy 3: Rebalance How We Tax Labor and Capital

As workers shift jobs, they need retraining and social safety net support. Working less means paying less income tax; government revenues drop when services are most needed. Funds must come from somewhere.

I believe we should tax AI tokens and robots. Currently, hiring someone means paying payroll taxes. Buying a robot can be written off as immediate business expense. Tax system nudges toward replacing people with machines.

A tax would slow automation slightly and raise money for retraining and stronger safety nets. It must target purely beneficial AI uses like making medicine and education cheaper.

Critics say it's not economically optimal, but they're not considering work's broader individual and societal value. With accelerated innovation, we can afford little inefficiency to keep people employed.

I proposed robot tax years ago; reaction was it was strange. I'm still a big proponent. Though not a complete solution, it's part of wise response. Regardless of funding method, money must reach those needing it most—AI/robot displaced workers, wage-declined people, concentrated-loss communities. Start now so systems are ready.

Moving Forward

What I'm Doing

  • Committing time and platform to making AI and equity a global priority
  • Advocating directly with lawmakers and world leaders for comprehensive policy frameworks
  • Gates Foundation will deploy AI to accelerate core missions—vaccines, healthcare, agriculture
  • Ensuring advances reach low-income countries where need is greatest
  • Breakthrough Energy will harness AI to solve climate challenges and clean energy access
  • Writing regularly to broaden conversation beyond technologists
  • Inviting workers, educators, religious leaders, and community members into shaping our AI future

I will use my voice and time to get AI and equity higher on the public agenda. I will raise this with lawmakers every D.C. visit and global leaders. It will be front-center in conversations with AI model developers. I'll advocate for the national and international framework described. Gates Foundation will drive beneficial usage, including in Africa. Breakthrough Energy will use AI helping companies develop cheap clean energy and solve climate problems. I'll write about AI regularly.

My Message to Leaders

You have a chance to act now, before unemployment rises sharply, communities hurt, and public trust erodes. Ensure your government handles the problem holistically, not dividing it into bureaucratic fiefdoms. Ensure AI benefits everyone. Work with other governments meeting national and global challenge.

I'll widen the circle shaping this debate. It should include workers, students entering workforce, community leaders, religious leaders, faith organizations, parents, educators, and others whose voices aren't typically heard but who have insight into transition effects on people's lives.

The Critical Questions

How do we ensure that the benefits of AI reach people who do not already have wealth, influence, and access?

How do we strengthen the social safety net and help workers and communities thrive even when they're displaced?

How should public institutions adapt?

And how do we preserve our humanity through all of this?

This unprecedented technology demands an unprecedented global response. If we get it right, the payoff for humanity will be phenomenal and the world will be a more equitable place.

I rarely stop thinking about AI—not because I have all answers, but because the questions it raises are too consequential to leave to small technologist groups. Leaders across academia, business, government, and civil society all have roles shaping what comes next.

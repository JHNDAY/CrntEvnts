A Tale of Two Hucksters

A senator with a list he never showed and a president with evidence always two weeks away. Seventy-six years apart, how much of the act is the same?

The premise. Two showmen, seventy-six years apart, built power on promises to expose a hidden enemy, with evidence that shrank whenever anyone checked. McCarthy's material comes from Halberstam's The Fifties, Chapter Three; Trump's from news reports as of October 2026.

Bottom line

The tactics rhyme: a promised revelation, a hidden enemy, a resentful audience, a frightened party. The differences are real too: McCarthy was one senator with no program, ended by televised hearings and censure in 1954; Trump has twice won the presidency and leads his party.

On February 9, 1950, in Wheeling, West Virginia, Senator Joseph R. McCarthy told a women's Republican club that he held a list of 205 Communists working in the State Department. He never produced it. In Chapter Three of The Fifties, David Halberstam calls him the accidental demagogue and describes four years of charges that changed American politics long after they collapsed. Donald J. Trump, now in his second term, has built a political career on similar promises of revelation, about a stolen election, about crime and immigration, about files that would expose a corrupt elite. This page sets the two side by side on eight measures. The title is the discussion's starting premise, not a settled verdict: each Trump column carries the case his supporters make, and the closing note says where the comparison breaks down.

I

The Promise to Reveal

McCarthy

The list was always about to appear. In Denver he said it was in a suit on the plane; the Denver Post captioned his photo "Left Commie List In Other Bag" H. In Reno, two reporters watched him on the phone with his staff, writing down names as fast as they came and asking for more. One entry read "Howard Shipley, HARVARD ASTR." McCarthy said it meant a Harvard astrologer. The reporter called Harvard and learned the man was Harlow Shapley, an astronomer H.

Pressed later that night at the Mapes Hotel bar, McCarthy searched his pockets, found no list, and accused the reporters of stealing it H. In two days the number had gone from 205 to 57 to 4 H.

Trump

The revelation has a familiar shape: big news coming soon. Before his July 16, 2026 primetime address, Trump had promised "big news" on election security; analysts said much of the speech repackaged old or unsupported material. He said the administration was releasing hundreds of pages of classified material that would show the largest compromise of election data in history.

Other promises followed the same arc. He campaigned on releasing the Justice Department's Epstein files, then resisted for most of 2025 and signed a release bill in November only after his own party forced the issue. A health-care plan "in two weeks" became a running phrase across both terms.

Supporters' viewTrump did force the Epstein files out over establishment resistance, and the election material he released raised real questions that the press refuses to examine.
II

Contempt for the Elites

McCarthy

Halberstam's McCarthy was the poor Irish kid from the wrong side of the tracks in Appleton, and he burned with the same resentments he sensed in his audiences H. Class distinctions mattered more to him than Communism. He hated the snobbery, real or imagined, of men like Dean Acheson and Alger Hiss H.

"bright young men with silver spoons in their mouths"McCarthy, early post-Wheeling speech, as quoted by Halberstam H

His home state answered Eastern criticism in kind. A Wisconsin editor told New Yorkers and Easterners that whom Wisconsin sent to the Senate was none of their business H.

Trump

Trump is no poor kid; he is a Wharton graduate and the heir to a Queens real-estate fortune. But he grew up an outer-borough developer looked down on by Manhattan society, and he turned that grievance into a politics. His targets are "the swamp," "the deep state," the press he calls "the enemy of the people," Ivy League universities, and career civil servants.

Like McCarthy, he aims less at a policy than at a class: the experts, editors and officials who, in his supporters' telling, look down on ordinary Americans.

Supporters' viewThe condescension is real. Elite institutions did dismiss working-class voters, and those voters are right to resent it. Trump is the first politician in decades to take their side.
III

The Desire to Be Accepted

McCarthy

The Reno reporter left convinced the anti-Communism was a con, and that only one thing about McCarthy was real: his wish to be a pal H. He greeted strangers with an arm around the shoulders, invited reporters back to his room for whiskey, and could not wait to dine with them after berating witnesses all day H.

Halberstam reads the heavy drinking as a mask for deep insecurity: he was almost pathetically eager to please H. Senator Paul Douglas, attacked and then befriended, compared him to a mongrel that fawns one moment and bites the next H.

Trump

Trump's need for acceptance runs through the record: the crowd sizes, the ratings, the hunger for approval from the New York society and press that long kept him at a distance. He courts reporters he denounces, calls them personally, and prizes coverage of any kind.

Admirers and critics alike describe the same pattern Douglas saw in McCarthy: lavish praise for those who flatter him, and sudden public attack on allies who stray.

Supporters' viewEvery politician wants approval. What matters is that Trump keeps the promises his voters care about, whatever his personal style.
IV

Disregard for the Facts

McCarthy

His skill, Halberstam writes, was to imply far more than he said. Were the named men Communists, sympathizers, or simply people who had "furthered the purpose" of Communism? He would not say H.

"Talking to Joe was like putting your hands in a bowl of mush."George Reedy, United Press H

When a Madison editor demanded he name one Communist at the paper, McCarthy sat in silence for fifteen minutes and walked out H. After the attack on the Army, Senator Ralph Flanders mocked him for returning with the scalp of a pink dentist H.

Trump

Fact-checkers have logged false or misleading claims from Trump by the thousands. The central one, that the 2020 election was stolen, failed in more than 60 lawsuits, recounts and audits, and in review by his own Justice Department.

The method resembles McCarthy's: say enough to alarm, never enough to pin down. In 2024 he repeated a debunked rumor that Haitian immigrants in Springfield, Ohio were eating pets. Pressed on such claims, he often says only that "people are saying" them.

Supporters' viewThe fact-checkers are partisan, and the press got big stories wrong too, from the Russia dossier to the origins of COVID. Trump's exaggerations point at real problems.
V

The Weapon: An Enemy Within

McCarthy: Communists in the government

The charge worked because it explained frightening events with a simple story. China had not fallen to history; it had been lost by traitors. If the world went wrong, Halberstam writes, something conspiratorial must have happened H. Behind it lay the real fears of 1949: the Soviet bomb, the Hiss case, the Fuchs spy ring.

The evidence was thin. McCarthy had few names of his own; most were fed to him by J. Edgar Hoover's FBI, and most were fellow travelers from the 1930s H. Halberstam doubts he could have recognized a real spy ring H. Yet the weapon worked: Democrats spent thirty years proving they were not soft on Communism, a fear that followed Lyndon Johnson into Vietnam H.

Trump: stolen votes and dangerous immigrants

Trump's version also explains a painful event, his 2020 defeat, with a hidden enemy. By mid-2026 he was raising election fraud more than once a day on average, and that January FBI agents had seized hundreds of boxes of 2020 records from Fulton County, Georgia, reviving claims investigators had already rejected.

The evidence again shrinks under examination. In Maricopa County, a federal database flagged 60 registered voters as noncitizens who had cast ballots; the county attorney's investigators found 52 of them were citizens, leaving 8 noncitizen voters across elections from 2012 to 2025. The parallel claim, that immigrants drive violent crime, also runs against the research: studies consistently find immigrants are not more likely than native-born Americans to commit crimes.

Supporters' viewEven a few illegal votes matter in close races, and state audits using the federal database have found hundreds of noncitizens on the rolls in states such as Georgia and Louisiana. Border crossings were high under Biden, and crimes by people who should have been deported are real tragedies.
VI

The Audience: Hammer's Readers and the MAGA Faithful

The Mike Hammer crowd

Halberstam closes the chapter with Mickey Spillane, whose private eye Mike Hammer sold in the millions at a quarter a paperback H. Hammer was the honest man who had given up on corrupt cops, crooked DAs and bought judges, and took justice into his own hands, "I'm the jury now, and the judge" H. By 1951 he had stopped chasing gangsters and started killing Communists H.

One historian of the paperback boom called Hammer the McCarthyite soul of the country, a superman for frightened Americans H. Critics despised Spillane. He answered that the critics were Losers who resented Winners, and only his readers counted H.

The MAGA movement

The echoes are striking. Trump's movement shares Hammer's premise that the official system (courts, prosecutors, the FBI, the press) is rigged, and that one strong man must set it right. "I am your retribution," Trump told a 2023 conservative gathering.

Spillane's own vocabulary, Winners and Losers, is Trump's. So is the reply to critics: the experts are failures who resent success, and only the audience matters. Both found mass audiences through a new medium the elites underrated, the 25-cent paperback then and social media now.

The comparison in briefHammer's readers were not all McCarthyites, and Trump voters are not a single type. Many are drawn by prices, jobs, the border or abortion rather than by the grievance show. Their anxieties, like the Cold War fears of 1950, are real even when the stories offered to explain them are not.
VII

The Cowed Establishment

Taft and the Republicans of 1950

The isolationist "primitives," as Acheson called them, rallied to McCarthy as expected H. The surprise was Robert Taft of Ohio, perhaps the most principled Republican in the Senate and a good man on civil liberties H. Wheeling caught him off guard, but he saw that the issue worked. If one case failed, he advised, McCarthy should keep talking and try another H.

Privately uneasy, Taft publicly praised McCarthy as a fighting Marine. Richard Rovere compared him to an alcoholic fighting the bottle H. The cost of resisting was clear: Millard Tydings, who called the charges a hoax, lost his seat in 1950 to a McCarthy-backed campaign H. Not until 1954 did the Senate censure him, 67 to 22.

Today's Republicans in Congress

Most of Trump's would-be critics in his party left or lost. Of the ten House Republicans who voted to impeach him in 2021, only two remained in Congress after 2022. Senator Thom Tillis announced his retirement in 2025 after opposing Trump's signature tax bill and drawing his threat of a primary.

The fear is lifting only where it no longer applies. In 2026, at least nine Republican senators who will not return to the chamber have begun breaking with Trump on key votes, a group nicknamed the "YOLO conference." Bill Cassidy, after losing his primary to a Trump-backed challenger, voted with Democrats to advance a bill limiting Trump's war powers, and John Cornyn also lost his primary.

Supporters' viewRepublicans back Trump because their voters do. Supporting a popular party leader is democracy working, not cowardice.
VIII

The Press as Amplifier

McCarthy

Halberstam calls the press corps the real scandal. Reporters knew better, but McCarthy was news, so they followed the road show from charge to charge without staying behind to check H. He timed his charges to their deadlines and made them in small towns, where a local wire stringer would send them out unchecked H.

The Chicago Tribune's Willard Edwards said McCarthy kept him on page one for four years, and with his paper's permission he supplied names and drafted speeches H.

Trump

Cable networks aired Trump's 2015–16 rallies live and uncut because they drew ratings; one network chief said his candidacy was bad for the country but good for business. Partisan outlets and podcasters now play the role Edwards played, helping shape the message they report.

Trump feeds the cycle much as McCarthy did, with a new charge each day so that yesterday's is never checked. In his July 2026 address he called parts of the media part of a plot and suggested their broadcast licenses be revoked.

McCarthy's fall came fast once the cameras turned on him, during the televised Army hearings of 1954. Whether anything similar awaits a sitting president, with his own party's base still largely behind him, is the open question.

For Discussion
  1. McCarthy's number fell from 205 to 4 in two days, yet the charge kept working for four years. Why do shrinking numbers so rarely end a scare?
  2. Halberstam says McCarthy did not really care about Communists. Does it matter whether a politician believes his own charges?
  3. Spillane told critics they were Losers who resented Winners. Where do you hear that argument today?
  4. Taft privately disliked McCarthy and backed him anyway. What would it have taken for him to break, and what would it take for Republicans now?
  5. Is "huckster" fair to both men, either, or neither?
A note on fairness

The analogy has limits. McCarthy was one senator with no program beyond headlines; Halberstam says he had no plan even for tomorrow's lunch. Trump has won the presidency twice, commands a party, and has a legislative and judicial record his supporters credit.

McCarthy is judged with seventy years of hindsight; Trump's record is still being written, and his voters' grievances deserve weight on their own terms. Current figures come from news reports and polling as of October 2026 and may change.

News in Review

October 4 – October 10, 2026 · Full Analysis
Where Things Stand

Polling data, the unpopular Trump's active role in the campaign, and the defensive posture of the Republican Party and its candidates all point to a favorable outcome for the Democrats, who appear very likely to gain control of the House and appear to have a better-than-even chance of taking the Senate as well.

That said, Trump has impugned election integrity on 70% of the days of his second term, and just this week his government declined to invite the Organization for Security and Cooperation in Europe to observe our federal elections, as it has done every two years since 2002. Notwithstanding the specter of pre- and post-election shenanigans, hopefully the iceman cometh to end Republican control of Congress. Here is where things stand regarding the House, the Senate, and gubernatorial races.

The House

The partisan split is currently 220 R and 215 D (assigning the two vacancies to the party that held them). The WAPO reported this week that Democrats are targeting 46 (21%) of the Republican seats. On Friday, the Cook Political Report moved 18 House seats toward the Democrats and predicted that the most likely outcome was a Democratic gain of between 9 and 19 seats.

The projected flips of 8 Democratic seats above reflect the impact of Republican gerrymanders, which had been expected to pick up about 10 seats on their own. Undoubtedly the 15 current Republican seats now rated as toss-ups include some where the gerrymanders went awry. By way of illustration, consider four congressional districts in a state, three of which are R+10 and one D+10, gerrymandered into four districts that are all R+5. Given the 8–9% advantage Democrats currently hold on the generic ballot, any of the four new districts could conceivably go Democratic.

What the ratings imply. If the ratings were all exactly correct and the Toss-ups split equally, the new House would be 223.5 D to 211.5 R, an 8.5-seat net flip consistent with the lower end of Cook's range. If Democrats win 75% of the Lean D's, ⅔ of the Toss-ups and 50% of the Lean R's, they reach 228.5 seats, a 13.5-seat net flip. Reaching the 19-seat net flip at the upper end of Cook's range would take about 5 more seats shifting toward Democrats, which is entirely feasible in this climate.

The Senate

Republicans currently hold a 53–47 advantage, with 22 R and 13 D seats up for election, leaving 31 R and 34 D seats intact. On the surface that looked like a great opportunity for Democrats from the outset, but most of the Republican seats were in deep Red territory. The initial assessment was that about 44 D and 47 R seats were assured, with 9 competitive races left to decide the incoming Senate. It soon became clear, however, that Roy Cooper would likely flip the R seat in NC and that Jon Ossoff would hold his D seat in GA, bringing the count to 46 solid D versus 47 R, with the 7 competitive seats we've been tracking for a few months.

New Hampshire moves to Likely D. This week I'm making a change that conforms with, but is not based upon, Cook's ratings. Democrat Chris Pappas has settled into about a 6% lead over John Sununu, and, in small part, the Sununu commercials in which the 15-year lobbyist criticizes career public servant Pappas for being in the pocket of lobbyists irritate me. That brings our count to 47 seats for each party. A sidebar: if Sununu won, his 18-year hiatus from the Senate would be the longest such gap for a re-elected senator in modern American history.

Kansas becomes competitive. We've all heard about deep Red seats becoming competitive, and now it has happened in Kansas. The Democratic candidate is mega-church pastor Adam Hamilton, a registered Republican until this year, running against first-term Republican Roger Marshall. The most recent polls show a dead heat in a state that has not elected a Democratic senator since 1932, and two former Republican Kansas governors recently endorsed Hamilton. That changes our count to 47 D versus 46 R.

Governors

In 2017–18, the number of Republican governors peaked at 33, against 16 Democrats and 1 Independent. Democrats have since increased their share, and the current count is 26 R to 24 D. This year there are 36 gubernatorial races. Controlling state governments is of particular importance now because they can mitigate the excesses of the Trump administration.

Four noteworthy debates this week:

Georgia. Jon Ossoff told Mike Collins, “you hired half the Third Reich to staff your congressional office,” to which Collins had no cogent response. Collins hewed closely to Trump and tried to tie Ossoff to Biden, whom he called the root cause of all our current problems. Collins appears to have squandered any chance he had to make the race more competitive.

Iowa. Josh Turek accused Ashley Hinson of enabling Trump, particularly on tariffs and the Iran War. Hinson, also a good debater, separated herself somewhat from Trump and accused Turek of being an out-of-touch liberal. Given Iowa's aging population, they debated Social Security: Hinson advocated raising the retirement age, while Turek called for removing the cap on taxable wages. There was no clear winner.

Maine. In two debates between Troy Jackson and Susan Collins, neither was a skilled debater, leading James Pindell to call the first “pretty much a mess” in the Globe. Collins was in the difficult position of separating herself from Trump, with whom she votes 95% of the time, made harder by the damage the tariff war with Canada is doing to Maine's economy. She took credit for getting “concrete, pulp and road salt removed from the tariff list,” but Jackson replied, “We can't eat road salt.” The debates were probably not impactful, but it may have helped Jackson to hold his own.

Michigan. An extremely contentious debate: Mike Rogers stressed that Abdul El-Sayed is a Muslim and tried to associate him with terrorism. El-Sayed depicted Rogers as an elitist who owns two yachts and a flip-flopper, and Rogers may have illustrated the latter point by going out of his way to voice newfound opposition to ICE, the Iran War and tariffs. Rogers' angry mien contrasted with El-Sayed's glib composure.

Beyond the Midterms

A number of noteworthy non-election items stood out this week:

  • Trump's over-the-top chagrin at not having received the Nobel Peace Prize again.
  • Pete Hegseth's announcement that Ft. Hood shooter Nidal Hasan would be executed by firing squad, and that it would be livestreamed.
  • The disclosure that our Ambassador to Greece, Kimberly Guilfoyle, asked a Republican donor to pay her $100k Amex bill in exchange for access to the administration.
  • Continuing ICE shootings, and an increase in ICE's daily arrest quota from 2,000 to 3,000 per day.
  • The reopening of the Lisa Cook investigation, on trumped-up charges of mortgage fraud.

The Fifties and Now

David Halberstam opens The Fifties with a country that was prosperous, powerful and angry. How does the political mood of 1946–1952 compare with the United States in October 2026, three weeks before the midterms?

Halberstam's first chapter is not really about the 1950s at all. It is about the years just before, when a party shut out of the White House since 1932 decided the high road had failed, and the hunt for internal enemies became the organizing force of American politics. Dewey lost in 1948, the Hiss case broke, China fell, and a "mean time" set in.

Seventy-five years later, the roles look partly reversed and partly familiar. The party in power is dominated by a single figure, the opposition is energized and divided, a foreign war is souring the public, and Americans' trust in Washington sits near historic lows. This page sets the two moments side by side, theme by theme, and marks where history echoes and where it breaks.

Theme 1

The towering figure

Both eras are organized around one man whose personality defines the political map. The difference is where he stands.

1949 The shadow of FDR

Roosevelt had been dead four years, yet Halberstam says leading Republicans privately feared their party might be a permanent minority. The Depression and the war had rebuilt the electorate around him, and his mastery of radio made him feel like a personal guide to millions of poorer and immigrant Americans.

Dewey and Eisenhower concluded in 1949 that no candidate who openly opposed "national planning" could win. The Republicans needed a popular figure who had taken no stand at all, and would govern differently once elected.

2026 The presence of Trump

Trump is not a shadow. He is the sitting president and the center of his party. A Polish Institute of International Affairs analysis in July judged the GOP still firmly dominated by Trump, with MAGA controlling Republican primaries.

30%approve of Trump's job performance (Pew, Sept. 28–Oct. 4)
67%of his own 2024 voters approve, down from 95% early in the term
21%confident he can bring the country closer together

Pew found his rating now comparable to the lowest point of his first term.

Echo
In both eras one personality defines what each party is for or against. In 1949 the Republicans were defined by opposition to Roosevelt; today Democrats are largely defined by opposition to Trump.
Break
FDR's shadow was a source of strength for his party. In 2026 the dominant figure is unpopular with the wider public while still commanding his party's base, which is an unusual combination.
Theme 2

Parties at war with themselves

Halberstam's richest material is the Republican civil war. Both parties today have their own versions.

1949 East vs. heartland

  • Eastern Republicans: Wall Street lawyers and bankers, New York media, internationalist, resigned to parts of the New Deal. Leaders: Willkie, Dewey.
  • Heartland Republicans: small-town businessmen, wary of Europe and Britain, nostalgic for the 1920s, reading Colonel McCormick's Chicago Tribune. Leader: Robert Taft.
  • The heartland saw the Easterners as traitors who had made peace with the New Deal.
  • Democrats were split too, between Northern urban liberals and the Jim Crow South, but winning held them together.

2026 Loyalists vs. dissenters; progressives vs. establishment

  • Republicans: the Iran war split the "America First" wing. Commentators such as Tucker Carlson and Rep. Marjorie Taylor Greene publicly broke with the president over it. Analysts say these cracks are real but have not formed an organized counterweight to Trump.
  • Democrats: primaries in New York, Michigan and Colorado pitted establishment candidates against insurgents, some aligned with the Democratic Socialists of America. Schumer answered that a big tent is a strength.
  • Research cited by Euronews suggests the insurgent wins reflect appetite for economic populism and frustration with party leaders more than a wholesale move left.
Echo
The Taft wing's suspicion of foreign entanglements reappears in today's MAGA dissenters, who object to a war in the Middle East. Then as now, the insurgents accused their own party's leaders of betraying its true principles.
Break
In 1949 the isolationists were outside the White House attacking it. In 2026 the president leads the restraint-minded wing's party and launched the war it opposes, so the fight runs through his own coalition.
Theme 3

The politics of getting even

Halberstam says postwar Republican rhetoric had one purpose and tone: getting even. Grievance and retribution are again central words in American politics.

1949 Out of power, out for revenge

After 1948, Halberstam writes, the bitterness inside the GOP grew, and the Communist issue became fair game. Dewey's refusal to red-bait had lost; the party concluded it was the only way it knew to fight back.

The instrument was Congress. HUAC, which Halberstam describes harshly, pursued Alger Hiss with a junior congressman, Richard Nixon, pressing on when others wanted to drop the case. The target behind Hiss was the New Deal itself, and soon Dean Acheson.

2026 Retribution, and the argument over it

During the 2024 campaign Trump spoke of punishing the "enemy from within." NPR counted more than 100 such threats since 2022. Trump and his allies argue this answers the four criminal cases brought against him, which they call political persecution.

Since 2025 the Justice Department has charged or investigated a number of the president's critics. Protect Democracy, a pro-democracy advocacy group, lists 36 cases it considers retaliatory. Supporters see these as overdue accountability.

Meanwhile, the out-party's energy runs the other way: Pew finds Democratic voters more engaged in the midterms.

Echo
Both eras feature a deep sense among one side that the other used government against it, and a resulting appetite to settle scores.
Break
In 1949 the out-party used congressional hearings against the executive branch. Critics in 2026 say the pressure flows from the executive branch toward opponents, a reversal of direction that matters for how checks and balances work.
Theme 4

The enemy label

In each era a single word does a lot of political work by turning an opponent into something un-American.

1946 "Communism vs. Republicanism"

"On one side are the Americans, on the other are the Communists and Socialists."George Murphy, at a GOP fundraiser, as quoted by Halberstam

RNC chairman B. Carroll Reece framed 1946 as a choice between Communism and Republicanism. "Socialism" in civic-club speeches covered almost any part of the New Deal. Chambers himself came to treat liberalism as a cousin of Communism, a habit Sidney Hook criticized.

2026 "Socialist," "enemy within," "fascist"

In September the House voted on a resolution condemning socialism and the Democratic Socialists of America. Republicans point to progressive primary winners as proof that the Democratic Party is moving toward socialism.

The labeling is not one-directional. Many Democrats and commentators describe the administration as authoritarian or fascist, and Trump has blamed opponents' rhetoric for political violence, including the 2025 killing of activist Charlie Kirk.

Echo
"Socialist" is doing a job in 2026 similar to the one it did in 1946: attaching mainstream opponents to their most radical allies.
Break
In 1949 there was a real foreign power, the Soviet Union, with real agents in the U.S. government. Today's labels mostly describe domestic rivals, and both sides use existential language about the other.
Theme 5

War and the public

Each era's mood was shaped by a foreign conflict that did not end the way Americans hoped.

1949 Won the war, lost the peace

America left World War Two richer and stronger than ever, yet the world felt closer and more threatening. Halberstam argues many Americans needed a scapegoat for Soviet control of Eastern Europe and the fall of China, and found one in Yalta, Hiss and Acheson.

Kennan's blunt 1946 view, that the U.S. could do little more than talk in Eastern Europe, was something many Americans could not accept.

2026 A war without a rally

U.S.-Israeli strikes on Iran began February 28, 2026. A University of Maryland review found no rally-round-the-flag effect reaching majority support.

57%say the Iran war is not worth fighting (Change Research, Aug.)
32%say it is worth fighting
48%think it could go on indefinitely

Republicans remain more supportive than Democrats and independents.

Echo
Frustration with an outcome abroad feeds blame at home. Then the question was "who lost China?"; now voters tie the war to gas prices and affordability.
Break
In 1949 the public broadly backed confronting the Soviets; the fight was over who was soft. In 2026 much of the public doubts the war itself.
Theme 6

Polls and certainty

Halberstam's account of 1948 is a lasting warning about political forecasting.

1948 "Dewey Defeats Truman"

Dewey campaigned as if he were already president. George Gallup told Republican leaders that further polling was a waste of money. Truman called Congress back into session to expose the GOP's conservative wing and won.

The Chicago Tribune printed its famous wrong headline, and Alistair Cooke had already filed a piece about Truman's failure.

2026 A Democratic tide, with a cushion

Republicans hold narrow majorities in both chambers, and NPR reports polling and primary results pointing toward possible GOP losses in both. Voters cite the Iran war, immigration, tariffs, gas prices and affordability.

Republicans have responses: new district maps that could add as many as 12 seats, a first-ever midterm convention in Dallas, and a campaign built around Trump rather than distance from him.

Echo
Expectations harden early in both eras. 1948 shows that a confident consensus can be wrong.
Break
Polling today is far more extensive than in 1948, and midterms are decided district by district, where map-drawing can matter as much as national mood.
Theme 7

Trust and mood

"It was a mean time," Halberstam writes. Both eras were anxious, but they started from very different levels of faith in government.

1950s Anxious but trusting

73%trusted Washington to do right always or most of the time (1958)

The anxiety was about the world and about hidden subversives. Faith in the institutions themselves stayed high, which made accusations of infiltration all the more shocking.

2026 Sour and distrustful

18%trust the federal government all or most of the time (Marquette, June)
4.2%annual inflation, May 2026

Pew describes Americans as in a sour mood. Trust is now driven by which party holds power: Republicans now trust Washington more than Democrats do, a near-perfect flip from the Biden years.

Echo
Both are periods of prosperity mixed with fear, where many people feel the country is slipping from their control.
Break
The 1950s anxiety was aimed at enemies inside trusted institutions. In 2026 distrust of the institutions themselves is the baseline.
Theme 8

Media

Halberstam treats communications technology as a political force in its own right.

1949 Radio and the partisan paper

FDR mastered radio. The heartland read McCormick's Tribune almost as scripture. The 1948 Dewey–Stassen radio debate drew a huge audience and foreshadowed televised debates. Bob Taft read the election from whether his wife still listened to Dewey.

2026 Platforms and personalities

News reaches people through podcasts, social feeds, cable and online influencers, each with its own audience. Commentators like Tucker Carlson can shape party fights from outside any office, much as McCormick did, but with far more competition.

Echo
The Tribune was a forerunner of today's partisan media: an outlet that tells one faction who the traitors in its own party are.
Break
1949 had a few dominant outlets and a shared set of facts. 2026 has many, and Americans often disagree on the basic facts.
Theme 9

Principle vs. expediency

Two of Halberstam's figures paid a price for refusing the popular move. Their stories frame a question for today rather than an answer.

1948–50 Dewey and Acheson

"You can't shoot an idea with a gun."Dewey, opposing a ban on the Communist Party, 1948

Dewey refused to red-bait even under heavy pressure, and lost. Acheson said he would not turn his back on Hiss, citing Matthew 25. Halberstam calls it brave and politically disastrous; Nixon used it at once.

2026 The same test, new names

Officials in both parties face the same choice: break with their side's base on a matter of conviction, or hold the coalition together. Republican dissenters over the Iran war and Democratic leaders weighing their progressive wing are both living some version of it.

Whether today's dissenters will be remembered like Dewey and Acheson, or not at all, is a fair question for discussion.

Echo
Then and now, defending an unpopular principle costs politicians in the short run.
Break
Halberstam writes with hindsight. We can't yet know how 2026's choices will look in 75 years.
Where the analogy fails

Five key differences

  1. Direction of pressure. In 1949 Congress investigated the executive. In 2026 the main concern critics raise runs from the executive toward opponents, while supporters argue it corrects earlier abuses.
  2. A real adversary. Soviet espionage was real, even if exaggerated for politics. Today's "enemy" rhetoric is aimed mostly at fellow Americans.
  3. Trust. The 1950s began with high faith in government. 2026 begins with very little.
  4. Where the strong man stands. FDR was dead and admired. Trump is present, dominant in his party, and unpopular overall.
  5. A shared reality. Three networks and a few big papers once set a common agenda. Today's fragmented media lets each side build its own story.
For the group

Discussion questions

  1. Halberstam says Republicans turned to the Communist issue because it was "the only way they knew how to fight back." What tools are today's out-party Democrats choosing, and how do they differ?
  2. The Taft wing distrusted foreign entanglements. Is today's MAGA opposition to the Iran war the same instinct, or something new?
  3. Dewey lost while taking the high road. Does 1948 prove that restraint loses elections, or that Dewey was simply a poor candidate?
  4. Trust in government fell from about 73% to under 20%. What caused that, and can it come back?
  5. "Socialist" was used against New Dealers and is used against progressives today. When does a label inform voters, and when does it mislead them?
  6. If you could ask Halberstam one question about 2026, what would it be?

This comparison draws on a single chapter of Halberstam, who wrote from a liberal-leaning perspective in 1993, and on 2026 reporting and polling that may be revised. Polls measure opinion at a moment; the midterm results on November 3 will be a better test of the national mood.

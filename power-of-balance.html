Five Issues on the Scale

The Times editorial board grants that President Trump's unpopularity was the main engine of the Democratic sweep. Its question is narrower: how did Democrats persuade voters in places like Kansas, Ohio, South Carolina and Texas who had long ruled the party out?

The board's answer is a combination. Winners ran as economic populists and cultural moderates, a mix it says matches a public that leans center-left on economics and center-right on social issues.

Read closely, the argument describes more than a position on a left-right line. Its candidates balanced in three distinct ways, and the board insists that one common idea of balance, always splitting the difference, is a mistake.

This guide examines that claim, then tests it against those who read the same election differently.

Three Kinds of Balance

The editorial's examples sort into three separate moves.

1

Across issues

Lean left where the public leans left, on wages, taxes and health costs. Move toward the center where the public sits there, on the border, policing and voter ID.

2

Within an issue

Name what each side got wrong in the same breath. A candidate faults Biden's border record and Trump's enforcement tactics, then offers a third position.

3

Against one's own party

Both parties sit near 40% favorability, so distance is an asset. Iowa's Josh Turek advertised his hundreds of votes with Republicans and styled himself a "common-sense prairie populist."

Balance Is Not the Midpoint

The board parts company with centrists who assume the right answer always lies halfway between the parties. On the economy, it wants bold action: winning candidates proposed higher taxes on the wealthy, a higher minimum wage, broader Medicare and more breakup of health-care conglomerates.

It also names what remains unbalanced. Democrats, it says, still lack a comprehensive economic agenda, are too timid about building homes, and have no plan for the federal debt.

And it treats the two parties as unequal: in its view, one supports democracy and the other has enabled autocratic behavior. For the board, balance is a strategy for beating that party. Republicans would reject that description outright.

Testing the Claim

The same election, read three other ways.

From the Left

It was the economics. A Data for Progress post-election report found voters tied Democrats to economic populism, while centrist messages registered far less.

Moderation may add little. Answering a similar Times editorial last year, political scientist Adam Bonica argued the data showed no extra seats from running more moderates.

From Analysts

Turnout, not persuasion. Brookings finds the sweep owed less to Republicans switching sides than to discontented Republicans staying home.

Both wings won. Brookings adds that progressives and moderates both prevailed, leaving 2028 open. ODI cautions against reading this as moderation defeating populism.

From the Right

A vote against Trump. Republican strategist Dick Wadhams called the result anti-Trump, not pro-Democratic. Rep. Don Bacon pointed to Trump's unpopularity and weak Senate nominees.

A normal midterm, badly run. Writing in the Daily Wire, Ryan Girdusky blamed flawed candidates and a turnout gap. Republican insiders remain split on referendum versus turnout.

A note on the evidence. The editorial draws its lessons almost entirely from candidates who won. To show that balance caused those wins, one would also need Democrats who ran the same way and lost, and comparable candidates who ran differently. Two races it cites, Alaska and Mississippi, were also still awaiting final counts when it was written.

For Discussion

  1. When a candidate balances, who pays? Is giving ground on trans athletes a fair trade-off, or a sacrifice of a small and vulnerable group for electoral gain?
  2. Can a party sustain this balance once it must vote on bills, or does it work only in campaign ads?
  3. If Republicans stayed home rather than switched sides, what has balance actually proven?
  4. Is there an issue on which you would refuse to balance? What distinguishes it from those where you would?

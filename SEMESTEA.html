Six regional theaters are fusing into one, from the Sahel to the Bay of Bengal

Interests, not ideology, now draw the map

A historian of the region argues that old sectarian and civilizational divides are giving way to cold calculations of security, technology and money.

Sebag-Montefiore, author of The Cauldron, sees a cascade of new deals and alliances marking a new era. The Middle East's conflicts are no longer contained. They are merging with wars and rivalries in Europe, Central Asia, South Asia and Africa.

The dividing line he draws is less Sunni versus Shia, or West versus Islam, than a pro-Western, open camp (Saudi Arabia, Egypt, the U.A.E.) against Iran and its "resistance" network. Within that frame, states pick partners case by case.

No single power dominates. Turkey, Iran and Israel jostle for primacy; Saudi Arabia is rich but exposed; Pakistan is a new contender. All, he says, are weaker than they look, and small actors like the Houthis can move events far beyond their size.

His closing warning: if these separate fires connect, the result could be a wider war. His prescription is a steady, coherent United States.

The Six Converging Theaters

Shaded = the author's "most dangerous"
Theater I

Russia–Ukraine meets Iran–Gulf

Black Sea ↔ Persian Gulf

Russia backs Iran with targeting intelligence; Ukraine sells its drone-defense know-how to the Gulf monarchies. Two wars now share customers and enemies.

Linked wars
Theater II

Caucasus & Central Asia

Azerbaijan · Armenia · Kazakhstan · Uzbekistan

Turkey and Israel both court Azerbaijan. India arms Armenia, Pakistan's ally's enemy. Kazakhstan, edging away from Russia and China, joins the Abraham Accords.

Chain of rivalries
Theater III

The "Byzantine" Theater

Eastern Mediterranean

Erdoğan's Turkey projects Ottoman-style power into Southern Europe and Syria. Sea-border and gas disputes push Greece into partnership with Israel.

NATO ally vs. NATO ally
Theater IV

South Asia

India · Pakistan · Afghanistan

Two nuclear rivals with five wars behind them, the latest in 2025. Pakistan is also bombing the Taliban. India balances Israel, Iran and Russia at once.

Nuclear flashpoint
Theater V

East Africa & the Red Sea

Sudan · Somalia/Somaliland · Ethiopia · Eritrea

A new "scramble for Africa," led now by Middle Eastern powers chasing ports, gold and farmland. The Houthis hold the Bab al-Mandeb strait.

Neo-imperial contest
Theater VI

The Sahel

Mali to Sudan belt

Military juntas fight jihadists, with Russia replacing France as patron. A collapse could send militants into Nigeria or toward Libya and Egypt.

Failing states
Talk It Over
  1. Theater II is a chain of "enemy of my enemy" ties: India arms Armenia because Pakistan backs Azerbaijan's friend Turkey. Do chains like this keep the peace by balancing power, or spread a war once one link breaks?
  2. Middle Eastern powers are now competing for ports, gold and farmland in East Africa. How does this differ from the European scramble for Africa in the 1880s?

Four Big Ideas

Reaffiliation

Interests over identity

States now line up by security, technology and economics rather than religion or ideology. Alliances overlap: the Mecca Pact and the Abraham Accords coexist.

Nervous system

Small actors, big effects

The region's exposed nerves, its shipping lanes and pipelines, let a minor force like the Houthis shake the global economy.

Flashover

When fires join

The firefighter's term for a room igniting all at once. The danger is that Ukraine, Iran and India–Pakistan merge into one war.

SEMESTEA

A new name for a new map

South Europe, Middle East, Sahara, South Asia, East Africa. "Middle East," "West Asia" and "SWANA" all leave pieces out.

Talk It Over
  1. Can you think of a past "flashover," such as 1914, when separate conflicts merged into one war? What made it happen, and what has prevented it since?
  2. The Houthis show how a small force can shake the world economy. What would make the region's "exposed nerves," its shipping lanes and pipelines, less vulnerable?

For Discussion

  1. Is "interests over ideology" really new, or has the region always worked this way beneath the rhetoric?
  2. Iran's attacks have pushed Arab states toward both Israel and the U.S. Is that alignment durable, or does it end when the Iran war does?
  3. Which theater worries you most? The author picks South Asia. Would you?
  4. Can China keep buying oil from both sides while staying out of the fighting?
  5. The author calls for a "measured, coherent" America. Is the current U.S. approach coherent, as he partly suggests, or contradictory?
  6. Does renaming the region (SEMESTEA) clarify our thinking, or is it a historian's game?

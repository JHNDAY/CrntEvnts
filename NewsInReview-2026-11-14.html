Icarus and the Price of Ignoring Warnings

In Greek mythology, Daedalus made wings of feathers and wax so that he and his son Icarus could escape imprisonment on Crete. He warned his son not to fly too high, or the sun would melt the wax. Icarus ignored the warning and plunged to his death in the sea. Three events this week showed how much it matters to heed warnings.

Air Traffic Control: A Ground Stop on Friday the 13th

On Friday, fittingly the 13th, the nation's air traffic control system failed, forcing a ground stop that lasted over four hours, the longest since September 11, 2001. Fortunately there were no tragic incidents, but the timing bodes poorly for air travel with Thanksgiving less than two weeks away. The government has been working to modernize the system since 2007, and progress under both Republican and Democratic leadership has been glacial.

Secretary of Transportation and reality TV star Sean Duffy cavalierly blamed Congress for ignoring his repeated requests for more funding. He conveniently overlooked the fact that Republicans have controlled both the legislative and executive branches for nearly two years. After hearing his predecessor Pete Buttigieg discuss air traffic control on Friday, one observation is unavoidable: Sean Duffy is no Pete Buttigieg.

Nvidia: The Fall of the Chip King

On Thursday, Jensen Huang resigned as chief executive of Nvidia rather than face forced removal. Nvidia is the world's leading computer chip developer, and its $4 trillion market capitalization is the second highest of any company in the world. Trump had been warned last December about Huang's relationship with suspected Chinese spy Alice Huang. Even so, in April, after such sales had been shut down, Trump let Nvidia sell advanced AI chips to China, provided the U.S. government received 15% of gross revenue from those sales.

Scott Bessent and Marco Rubio, who warned Trump about the relationship, have been feuding with Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. Maureen Dowd delivered a scathing verdict on Saturday morning: Huang, Lutnick and Trump "aren't looking out for us" at a perilous moment for gaining control over A.I.

North Carolina: A Gerrymander Turned on Its Head

Republicans fared poorly in the midterms, and North Carolina is a particularly instructive case. Although it is a purple state, it is arguably the most gerrymandered in the nation, which produced a 10–4 Republican House delegation. That wasn't enough for Republicans. They drew a new map to flip the NC-01 seat of two-term Democratic Congressman Don Davis and produce an 11–3 split.

With three races still to be called, an 11–3 split now looks likely, but with Democrats holding the 11 seats. Those probably include the seat of 12-term Congresswoman and Rules Committee Chair Virginia Foxx. Democrats also succeeded in the state legislature, breaking a 16-year Republican hold on the state House. Control of the state Senate hinges on the lone uncalled race, where the Republican holds a narrow lead. A Democratic win there would bring joint leadership of the chamber.

The Democrats' 13% margin is the largest for either party since the 1974 post-Watergate midterms, and it exceeds the 9% generic-ballot consensus. Part of that gap reflects a late Democratic surge that polling missed, but that cannot explain much of the 4-point disparity.

NPR studied turnout in the six southeastern states other than Florida. It found turnout fell 19% in counties Harris won, compared with 26% in counties Trump won. Across these states, NPR measured a 22% swing toward Democrats, against a national swing it calculated at 14.5%. Majority-Black counties also had turnout about 10% higher than majority white, non-Hispanic counties. These findings help explain Scott Colom's surprising Senate win in Mississippi and Keisha Lance Bottoms's decisive win for Georgia governor after polls forecast a squeaker. They also help explain the Democrats' stunning success in North Carolina.

The House: The Largest Majority in a Generation

As of last Saturday, CNN had called 254 House seats for Democrats, including 44 flips, and 163 for Republicans, with only 3 flips. Since then, Democrats won 3 more, including 2 flips, and Republicans won 6, with no flips, bringing the totals to 257 D and 169 R. One Democratic flip was MAGA firebrand Lauren Boebert's seat in CO-04, where she lost to retired Rear Admiral Eileen Laubacher. Boebert's rear won't be missed by many of us. Meanwhile, Ilhan Omar, reviled by the president and his cronies, won MN-05 with 76.5% of the vote.

Nine races remain uncalled. Republicans appear poised to win 4. Democrats hold small leads of roughly 300 votes in 3. The other 2, both in Louisiana, go to runoffs after the combined Republican vote on Election Day far exceeded the Democrats' total. If the 7 current leaders prevail and Republicans win both runoffs, the final tally would be 260 D to 175 R. The 49 Democratic flips would account for 21% of the current 220 Republican seats. The 4 Republican flips, one of them in Louisiana, would account for under 2% of the current 215 Democratic seats.

Only Paul LePage's narrow win over progressive Democrat Matthew Dunlap in ME-02 was a legitimate Republican flip. Republicans will have won only 3 of their 15 gerrymandered seats, while Democrats won all 6 of theirs, a net D +3 across the 21 redistricted seats. Had this not been a wave election, the Republican gerrymanders might have worked out far better for them.

Holding 260 seats would give Democrats the largest majority either party has held since their 267 seats in 1991–92, just above their 257 seats in Obama's first term. Only 8 of those 260 Democrats will be DSA members. As of now, the House will have 136 women (117 D and 19 R), 60 Black members, all Democrats and down from 63, and 3 Native Americans (2 D and 1 R).

The Senate: Alaska Settles It at 56–44

As of last Saturday, Democrats had flipped 7 Republican seats, bringing the Senate to 54 D and 44 R. Two races were unresolved, in Mississippi and Alaska, with the Democrat ahead by just 0.3% in each. By Wednesday, Scott Colom was declared the winner in Mississippi. Provisional ballots favored him, pushing his lead toward 0.7%, and incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith conceded promptly.

Alaska supposedly remains too close to call. However, Democrat Mary Peltola's lead has grown steadily over the last three days as votes trickled in. She now leads Daniel S. Sullivan 49.9% to 46.0%, a margin of 11,200 votes, with only about 8,000 ballots outstanding, so Sullivan cannot win. Any grousing that Daniel J. Sullivan's candidacy cost Daniel S. the election is moot, since Daniel J. received fewer than 4,700 votes. We are calling the race for Peltola.

Republicans entered the midterms defending 22 seats, mostly in deep-red territory, to the Democrats' 13. Democrats flipped 9 seats while holding all of theirs, exactly reversing this class's split to 22 D and 13 R. That brings the Senate to 56 D and 44 R, the most seats either party has held since Bill Clinton's first term. Because senators serve six-year terms, these results give Democrats an enduring advantage, especially since Republicans must defend 18 seats in 2028 to the Democrats' 16.

On diversity, the incoming Senate will have 24 women, down from 27. Peggy Flanagan, Julia Stratton and Mary Peltola were newly elected in Minnesota, Illinois and Alaska. Susan Collins, Cindy Hyde-Smith and Darline Graham lost, Amy Klobuchar left to become governor, and Joni Ernst and Jeanne Shaheen retired. There will be 7 Black senators (6 D and 1 R), up from 5, with Colom and Stratton newly elected. Flanagan and Peltola will be the first Native American women senators.

Governors: Democrats Lead Three-Quarters of the Nation

Democrats Cindy Holscher in Kansas and Jonathan Kreiss-Tomkins in Alaska were declared winners this week, bringing the final tally to 30 Democratic governors and 20 Republican. Democrats held all 18 of their seats up this year and flipped 6 of the 18 Republican seats in play. Thirty governorships is their most since 1993, a dramatic rise from just 16 in 2018.

Democrats will hold the governorship in all 7 traditional presidential battleground states, 5 of them as trifectas. In the other two, Georgia and North Carolina, they will control one legislative chamber, as they will in Texas. Democrats will govern 75% of the U.S. population, 53% of it under trifectas, and 13 of the 14 most populous states, with Florida the lone exception.

There will be 5 Black governors (4 D and 1 R), the most ever; before these midterms, only 3 Black governors had been elected since Reconstruction. There will also be 18 women governors (15 D and 3 R) and one Native American, Deb Haaland of New Mexico.

Looking Forward: Discipline in Victory

Two cautionary notes have been sounded about the Democrats' performance. James Pindell of the Boston Globe described the result as more a rejection of Trump than an endorsement of a big, bold Democratic agenda. Jim Kessler of Third Way said, "We won because we were talking about issues that voters cared about more than issues progressives cared about."

In this week's NYT Conversation, Bret Stephens questioned whether Chuck Schumer should remain Majority Leader, wondering whether the country wants two Brooklyn congressional leaders squaring off against a president from Queens. Having spent my first 20 years in Queens, I know that unmellifluous western Long Island accent well and could do without it.

Beyond dealing with Trump, Stephen Miller and Jim Jordan, Democrats must grapple with several questions:

Mandate: How best to meet the expectations of voters who resoundingly gave them control of Congress?

Oversight vs. delivery: How to balance investigating the corruption and malfeasance of Trump and his cronies against addressing voters' concerns?

Ambition vs. pragmatism: What is the right balance between progressive goals and less expansive, meat-and-potatoes governance?

Communication: How to reach the public through all the noise from Trump and his acolytes?

From the Democratic side: Josh Shapiro said, "We now have the keys to the car and we have to drive it responsibly." Congresswoman Hillary Scholten (MI-03) urged, "Get to work, Congress. We do not want political theater, we don't want drama, we don't want infighting." Former Biden pollster John Anzalone wrote that Democrats need an economic agenda that helps working-class families, seniors and small businesses.

From the Republican side: Newt Gingrich said, "If the Democrats can discipline themselves to listen to the American people, they could create a new majority that could govern for quite a long time." Bill Stepien, Trump's 2020 campaign manager, said the night of the midterms "undoubtedly made the mechanics of winning in 2028 harder for Republicans."

Giving Trump the last word, he asked, "Does anybody really believe that this 'election' was honest? How could the polls be so wrong?" He then posted, defying belief, "THE DEMOCRATS ARE CORRUPT AND EVIL, and I am the exact opposite."

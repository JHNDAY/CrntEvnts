Geopolitics · Montefiore in the Wall Street Journal
A Region That Needs a New Name
John Day10:28–11:50
Our next topic is "The Old Middle East Is Gone." Fran sent me a very interesting Wall Street Journal article and suggested we post it on the portal and discuss it. To make sure no good deed goes unpunished, Fran volunteered to moderate. There's a bio of the author on the page: he's written many books and is clearly on the right, and he has interesting things to say about how the geopolitical landscape has changed.
Francesca Nannetti11:51–15:03
"Middle East" is itself a construct of the European and American geopolitical system in the 19th and 20th centuries: the east of Europe, divided into the Middle East and the Far East. Montefiore's premise is that the region is no longer about Sunni versus Shia or the West versus Islam. It's about a pro-Western version of Islam, represented by Saudi Arabia, Egypt and the UAE, in conflict with Iran and its allies and sympathizers.

The irony is that attacks on Arab states, by the Houthis and others, have made the U.S. presence all the more necessary. These nations don't really trust the United States now, but they still look to it for protection up to a point. And the attacks have made Israel, as he says, more strategically attractive to Arab powers than ever before.
Francesca Nannetti16:40–21:43
He lays out six maps, six converging theaters, that he sees as seminal to this change. The Abraham Accords are U.S.-brokered agreements establishing diplomatic, economic and security relations between Israel and several Arab countries, and now even a Central Asian state is interested in joining. There's also a NATO-like defense pact involving Pakistan.

Everything in this region is fluid. Go back 10 or 15 years and these states opposed Israel completely; now, behind the scenes and not so behind the scenes, they negotiate with it, because what they want most is technology and security, and Israel has shown it can defend itself. He also brings in the Sahel, the wide, shallow band across Africa from the Atlantic to the Red Sea, dry where it borders the Sahara in the north and savanna in the south, and powers never considered part of the picture before: India, China, Pakistan. India and Pakistan are mortal enemies, both nuclear powers, and both in the mix.

So he proposes a new name. He doesn't much care whether it sticks, but instead of "Middle East" it would bring together Southern Europe, the Middle East, the Sahara and Sahel, South Asia and East Africa.
Editor's Note
The Abraham Accords were signed in 2020, not the 1990s. The Central Asian country recently associated with joining them is Kazakhstan. The defense pact mentioned is the 2025 mutual-defense agreement between Saudi Arabia and Pakistan.
Montefiore's Thesis
  • The dividing line: pro-Western Arab states versus Iran and its network
  • Houthi and Iranian attacks pushed Arab states toward the U.S. and Israel
  • Six converging theaters, from the Sahel to South Asia
  • New entrants: India, China and Pakistan now part of the region's calculus
  • A proposed new name spanning Southern Europe to East Africa

AI Corner · Steve Rattner in the New York Times
Why Did Productivity Stall, and Can AI Restart It?
John Day34:30–36:45
"Why We Need AI" by Steve Rattner is tied up with John Maynard Keynes's hypothesis in the 1920s that technology would significantly reduce how much people work. Growth comes from more workers times more output per worker, and innovation raises output per worker. The problem is that productivity growth has been stuck below its postwar peak since the early 1970s. Is AI going to do anything about that? It's a larger question about how technology affects society, going back hundreds of years, but this may be a singular moment, with AI as an accelerant.

The first thing that caught my eye in his chart is 1950 to 1970: higher than 1970–90, higher than the 2010s, higher than the 2020s. Why would productivity rise so much then?
George36:45–37:06
After World War II we sent GIs to college, and there was wartime technical innovation, even harnessing the atom.
John Day37:06–38:59
That's a good bit of it. But much of the innovation goes back to the beginning of the 20th century, and it took a long time to integrate into business practice. When electric motors became available, it took factories a long time to move away from the water-driven spindles running through the middle of the mills. For any technology to affect the economy at large, it takes a while. So if AI does lift productivity, how will we know?
David Rattner38:59–39:16
It's probably not going to be in traditional goods but in the service industry, primarily, although it could affect manufacturing processes so that goods rise as well. I never really thought about it.
"For any technology to have an impact on society and the economy at large, it takes a while." John Day
The Productivity Puzzle
  • Growth = more workers × more output per worker
  • Productivity growth stuck below its postwar peak since the early 1970s
  • 1950–70 boom: GI Bill, wartime innovation, and decades-old technology finally absorbed
  • Lag effect: factories took decades to reorganize around electric motors
  • If AI helps, the gains may show first in services

AI Corner · Can Capitalism Be Sustainable?
Water Wheels, Data Centers and Cars That Last
Larry Schwirian39:23–40:11
I wonder about AI and sustainability. If we went back to water wheels, we'd be in better shape on sustainability than we are now. Conceptually, sustainability is almost diametrically opposed to capitalism, because sustainable design is about doing nothing to affect the Earth's ecosystem, and almost everything we're doing affects it.
John Day40:14–42:08
I don't know that I agree. Take the Toyota Production System. After World War II, Japan was bankrupt, and Toyota had to sell everything it made as soon as it made it. Out of that came its system, and the realization that reliable cars had market value. Through the '50s, '60s and '70s, Wall Street kept telling Toyota to adopt the American system: big inventories and inefficient cars that had to be replaced quickly. That evolution nearly put the American auto industry out of business, but now you buy a car that goes past 100,000 miles. That's a very sustainable product, and it was driven by capitalism. It's a more nuanced question.
Larry Schwirian42:29–43:06
Responding to George's question about solar and wind: I'm thinking about data centers that use huge amounts of water and are straining the power grid. The Southwest has had water problems for a long time and it's getting worse. With AI, it will get worse yet.
Sally Fleschner43:18–43:51
Maybe 20 years ago industry realized it's profitable to make a refrigerator that lasts 8 years, not 30. At least from what I've seen with appliances, the new business model is don't design something that lasts forever.
John Day43:54–47:00
That may be true in some sectors, not all. Toyota is very profitable, and its lodestone is reliable cars. It's hard to kill a Toyota. Honda followed; all the Asian manufacturers followed Toyota's lead. Look up W. Edwards Deming on YouTube: a quality specialist who comes across like an Old Testament prophet. In the early '50s he told GM they were making cars the wrong way. GM said, who are you? We're making more money than we know what to do with. So Deming went to Japan, and about 40 years later the U.S. auto industry was nearly bankrupt.
"It's hard to kill a Toyota." John Day
The Debate
  • Larry: sustainability and capitalism are near opposites
  • John: Toyota shows market competition can reward durability
  • Sally: much of industry now designs for replacement
  • AI data centers strain water supplies and the grid, especially in the Southwest
  • Deming's ideas, rejected by GM, helped build Japan's quality revolution

AI Corner · Keynes's 15-Hour Work Week
More Free Time, or More Income?
John Day~45:30–47:30
Over the last couple of hundred years, hours worked have dramatically decreased and GDP per capita has dramatically increased. That's the effect of technology. Keynes expected a 15-hour work week. If AI raises productivity again, should we take the gains as more free time or more income? You could want six cars and three homes and keep working as you are, or say you'd rather research something on your own and take a three-day week.
Hilary Kent47:34–48:06
If I worked 15 hours instead of 38, I might be paid for 15 hours only and have much less to spend. There's no guarantee that working less means the same pay. With AI, people are losing their jobs and their income.
David Rattner48:18–48:56
The choice is very age dependent. If you're young, you need money. If you're old, you need time. And I agree with Hilary: you get paid for production. There's only so much money circulating, and it's doled out based on the scarcity of your skill and your ability to produce something of value.
Sally Fleschner49:36–51:04
I'm a lawyer. I can only bill for my hours. For those of us who can only sell our time, I'm not sure we can work a 15-hour week. Lawyers do a lot more than research. If you save time, now you do 10 cases instead of three.
John Day & George49:54–51:12
John: Many reputable law firms are using AI to cut research time, which was a large source of billable hours, carefully and with triple-checking. It's not going away. Taking on more cases would be the firm expanding its revenue.

George: Or decreasing the number of lawyers.
Linda Fritz & Larry Schwirian51:15–52:35
Linda: You have to include robots in the equation; they're taking a lot of manufacturing jobs.

Larry: In architecture we drafted with T-squares. When computer drafting came in, the workload didn't shrink; we just drew a lot more. And if it gets down to 15 hours a week, what is everybody going to do with the rest of their time?
John Day52:36–54:11
That's an important cultural question. Keynes's prediction was more aspirational. People need goals to get up in the morning, and many of us derive purpose from work for a long period of our lives. That doesn't mean retirees lack purpose, as the Kents asked. Look at us: we attend classes, read, have discussions. It's self-directed purpose, without the meetings and the bureaucracy. We only lack purpose if we choose to.
Sally Fleschner54:12–54:57
Here, when you retire, you plan to stay busy, and I do. But my friends in Bosnia retire between 60 and 63 and say, why would I take courses? They have a very different idea of how to spend retirement. I'm not saying one is right or wrong.
"If you're young, you need money. If you're old, you need time." David Rattner
Time Versus Money
  • Hours worked down, wealth per person up, over two centuries
  • Hilary: shorter hours may simply mean smaller paychecks
  • Law: AI cuts research hours, threatening the billable-hour model; firms may take more cases or employ fewer lawyers
  • Larry: computer drafting meant more drawings, not less work
  • Purpose: from work during a career, self-directed in retirement; cultures differ

AI Corner · Winners, Losers and Rattner's Prescriptions
The Workers' Slice Shrinks as Profits Grow
John Day54:58–56:36
Technology has given us more personal wealth and more freedom. A hundred years ago people worked a god-awful number of hours just to sustain themselves. Today, someone with the discipline to separate themselves from work can have more free time. But look at Rattner's charts: GDP per person over the last 50 years, and corporate profits, which have gone way up. The workers' slice shrinks and profits grow.
George56:36–56:46
You've been showing mean statistics. Median statistics would be interesting to look at as well.
John Day56:46–61:14
Those are Rattner's choices, but the decline has political and technological causes. In the '50s, manual labor was much more valuable. When I was in high school, guys would walk out, go to the local GM plant, get a good-paying job bending metal, and be very happy. That's gone. Technology took it, union power atrophied, and the political winds shifted in favor of business.

AI is an exaggerated case. Training and data centers are expensive, but once built, a model runs millions of times and you charge every time. The potential to concentrate money in a few hands is tremendous.

Rattner's prescriptions: retraining for young workers, wage insurance for older displaced workers, and securing Social Security and Medicare, perhaps by taxing the AI titans, though you can hear the other side saying they'll take their companies elsewhere. His bottom line: "stop AI" and "no rules needed" are both wrong. AI should proceed and deliver its benefits, with significant rules and a commitment to contribute to the public wealth. The far-out idea is universal basic income.
David Rattner61:56–62:57
It's a real thing in medicine too, and we could talk about the economics of tokens; there are a million things to talk about. On my condominium board, dealing with insurance policies and reserve requirements, we've used Claude extensively to find problems in documents and ask the lawyers the right questions. We use it every day of the week. I agree with Rattner: there's a middle path. It's not the end of civilization, and it's not the be-all and end-all either. This could be an entire session, if not more.
John Day62:57–63:48
I don't want to break the cadence of Current Events, so I'm happy to host a separate session off-cycle for those who want to get into the weeds. Look this over and email us about which aspect you want to drill into. The page links to the original Times article, which I gifted, and a short bio of Rattner, a columnist I think you can trust to be writing in good faith.

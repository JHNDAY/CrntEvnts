Democratic Socialist Movements in America

From William Jennings Bryan to the Rise of the DSA: 135 Years of Left-Wing Political Movements

August 2026

Data & Analysis for Group Discussion

Democratic socialist movements in America have surged, receded, and surged again across more than a century, each wave shaped by the economic anxieties and labor struggles of its time. From William Jennings Bryan's agrarian populism of the 1890s to Eugene V. Debs' revolutionary Socialist Party of the early 1900s to today's resurgent Democratic Socialists of America, the left has periodically challenged the Democratic Party establishment—sometimes winning policy concessions, often facing suppression, but rarely achieving the systemic transformation its adherents sought.

What is striking about the current moment is not merely the size of the movement—the DSA has reached 120,000 members as of July 2026, surpassing the Socialist Party of America's 1912 peak of 118,000—but the strategy through which that movement operates. Unlike Debs, who ran third-party presidential campaigns in 1900, 1904, 1908, 1912, and 1920, today's democratic socialists have infiltrated the Democratic Party from within, winning primary elections and forcing mainstream Democrats to contend with their demands.

This shift raises a central historical question: Why did the Socialist Party of America collapse after its 1912 peak, only to lie dormant for 70 years? And what has enabled the DSA to not merely survive but thrive where its predecessor failed?

The current era differs fundamentally from the Socialist Party epoch: DSA operates as a pressure faction within the Democratic Party rather than a third-party threat. Whether this represents a strategic evolution or a co-optation remains the central debate.

The Populist and Socialist Party Eras (1890–1930)

William Jennings Bryan and Agrarian Fusion

The first wave of American left-wing populism centered not on industrial socialism but on agrarian grievance. William Jennings Bryan, the Democratic Party's presidential candidate in 1896, 1900, and 1908, mobilized farmers and workers around monetary policy—specifically the demand for "free silver," the unlimited coinage of silver to inflate currency and ease rural debt burdens. Bryan's 1896 platform garnered 47% of the popular vote, making him one of the closest challengers to an incumbent Republican in the nineteenth century.

What distinguished the Populist-Democratic fusion was its scale: millions of farmers and workers mobilized through a party apparatus. Yet this fusion proved unstable. By the 1920s, the Democratic Party had abandoned Populist economics and aligned with urban industrial interests, leaving agrarian socialists politically homeless.

The Socialist Party of America (1901–1972)

More radical than Bryan's Populists, the Socialist Party of America (SPA) emerged in 1901 with 13,000 members. Over the next decade, membership exploded. By 1912, the SPA reached 118,000 dues-paying members—a figure the DSA would not surpass until 2026.

"I'd rather vote for what I want and not get it, than for what I don't want and get it."
— Eugene V. Debs, 1920

Under the leadership of Eugene V. Debs, a former railroad organizer radicalized by the Pullman Strike of 1894, the Socialist Party achieved its greatest electoral success in 1912. That year, Debs received 901,000 votes—approximately 6% of the popular vote—a threshold unmatched by any third-party candidate in American history save Theodore Roosevelt's Progressive (Bull Moose) Party that same year.

Electoral success mirrored organizational growth. By 1912, the Socialist Party had elected 1,200+ members to local office in 340+ American cities. Socialists served as mayors, city councilmembers, and state legislators. The party's newspaper, the Appeal to Reason, circulated 500,000 copies per week.

The Socialist Party seemed poised to become a permanent fixture of American politics—similar to the British Labour Party or German Social Democrats—rather than an ephemeral protest movement.

Collapse and the Cold War (1920–1960)

Yet the Socialist Party's trajectory reversed sharply. Three forces converged to undermine it: World War I, the Russian Revolution, and Red Scare suppression.

During World War I, the Socialist Party split over whether to support the Allies. Pacifist Debs was imprisoned for his anti-war activism and ran for president in 1920 from his jail cell, receiving 919,000 votes. However, the war wounded the party's working-class base, many of whom enlisted or split on internationalism.

More fatally, the Russian Revolution (1917) split American socialism into competing factions: those who saw Soviet communism as the inevitable future and those who rejected Bolshevism. The Communist Party of America, founded in 1919, began to absorb the most radical SPA members. By 1947, the Communist Party had 75,000 members—surpassing the declining SPA.

The post-World War II Red Scare under McCarthy eliminated whatever remained of socialist organizational presence. The SPA formally dissolved its identity in 1972, rebranding as Social Democrats USA, a much smaller and more cautious organization.

Policy Victories Despite Political Defeat

Paradoxically, the Socialist Party's electoral collapse did not erase its policy influence. Many socialist demands—considered radical in 1912—were adopted by mainstream Democrats and Republicans by the 1930s:

  • 8-hour workday (1912 SPA demand) → Adopted via New Deal legislation (1938)
  • Child labor abolition (1912 SPA demand) → Federal law (Fair Labor Standards Act, 1938)
  • Women's suffrage (1912 SPA demand) → 19th Amendment (1920)
  • Progressive taxation (1912 SPA demand) → Implemented by FDR (1933+)
  • Minimum wage (1912 SPA demand) → Federal minimum wage (1938)

Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal absorbed much of the Socialist Party's agenda—or at least its surface language. The difference was crucial: Roosevelt implemented capitalist-friendly versions of these demands through government spending and regulation, not systemic transformation toward collective ownership.

Key Lesson: Co-optation Without Revolution

The SPA achieved policy victories while failing to build lasting political power. The Democratic Party and capitalist elites learned to absorb socialist rhetoric while rejecting socialist economics. By the 1950s, this pattern would repeat: accept the demands, reject the movement.

The Long Silence and DSA's Founding (1972–2015)

From 1972 to 2015, American democratic socialism was organizationally moribund. The Democratic Socialists of America, founded in 1982 through a merger of the Democratic Socialist Organizing Committee and the New American Movement, began with merely 6,000 members. For three decades, DSA remained a fringe organization, fluctuating between 5,000 and 10,000 members.

The Cold War had effectively liquidated the left. McCarthyism had created a political culture in which even labor unions distanced themselves from socialism. The Democratic Party aligned itself firmly with anti-communist labor leaders and foreign policy establishments. A minor member of Congress could be career-ending by association with socialism.

DSA's strategy during these decades differed fundamentally from the SPA's. Rather than running independent presidential candidates, DSA focused on issue advocacy—antiwar organizing, labor solidarity, Central American solidarity campaigns. The organization did not seek to build an independent electoral machine.

This strategy reflected the era's constraints. Independent third-party candidacies were electorally futile in a two-party system. Labor unions, once the foundation of working-class politics, had declined from 33% of the workforce (1950s) to 10.5% (2015). Socialism carried a stigma that would take decades to overcome.

Yet the conditions for revival were quietly forming. Income inequality had surged post-1980. Student debt had become endemic. The 2008 financial crisis had discredited mainstream economics. By 2015, a generational cohort of young people, burdened by debt and facing precarious labor markets, was becoming open to socialist ideas in a way their predecessors had not been.

The stage was set. All that was needed was a catalyst—a candidate who could credibly articulate these grievances within the Democratic Party apparatus.

The Sanders Effect and Democratic Socialist Resurgence (2016–2026)

That catalyst arrived in February 2015 when Senator Bernie Sanders of Vermont announced his candidacy for the Democratic presidential nomination. Sanders, an independent who had long described himself as a democratic socialist, brought decades of credibility and a platform that centered on wealth inequality, healthcare as a right, and opposition to American imperialism.

The 2016 Sanders campaign had three immediate effects: It legitimized democratic socialism within Democratic Party discourse; it energized a new generation of young activists; and it catalyzed DSA recruitment. By the time the November 2016 primary ended, DSA membership had climbed to ~8,500. By 2017, as the Trump presidency galvanized further left-wing organizing, DSA membership reached ~25,000.

Crucially, in late 2016 and early 2017, the DSA made a strategic decision: instead of running independent candidates, it would endorse and mobilize for progressive candidates seeking the Democratic Party nomination. This marked a decisive break from SPA precedent. Rather than flee the Democratic Party, DSA infiltrated it.

The Squad and Electoral Breakthrough

The most visible expression of this strategy was the 2018 election of Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez (AOC) to Congress from New York's 14th district. At 28, Ocasio-Cortez was a DSA member who had worked as an organizer. Her primary upset victory against a ten-term incumbent shocked the Democratic establishment and galvanized DSA recruitment. By 2020, DSA membership had surged to ~80,000.

The 2020 Democratic primary saw Bernie Sanders campaign again, this time with even more mainstream support. While Sanders did not secure the nomination (Joe Biden's campaign consolidated the establishment and older voters), the 2020 primary demonstrated that democratic socialist ideas had moved from fringe to mainstream within the Democratic Party base. Sanders won Iowa and New Hampshire, and came close in Nevada and California.

The years 2020–2024 saw a consolidation of DSA power within the House of Representatives. AOC and her allies in what the media termed "the Squad"—including Rashida Tlaib (Michigan), Ilhan Omar (Minnesota), Cori Bush (Missouri), and others—used their platforms to advance Green New Deal, healthcare-for-all, and anti-imperialism positions. Though a small minority within the House Democratic Caucus, they punched above their weight through media savvy and grassroots mobilization.

The 2026 Breakthrough and Establishment Backlash

The most dramatic recent moment in DSA electoral history came in 2025–2026. In November 2025, Zohran Mamdani, a 33-year-old DSA member and New York State Assemblyman, was elected Mayor of New York City after winning a contentious Democratic primary. Mamdani's campaign centered on affordable housing, labor organizing, and the slogan "Abolish ICE" (Immigration and Customs Enforcement).

Mamdani's election energized DSA recruitment and fundraising. By July 2026, DSA membership had reached an all-time high of 120,000+, officially becoming the largest socialist organization in American history.

In June 2026, just months after Mamdani's victory, DSA-endorsed candidates swept congressional primaries in New York and Colorado:

  • Melat Kiros (CO-08) unseated 30-year Democratic incumbent Diana DeGette
  • Darializa Avila Chevalier (NY-12) unseated Rep. Adriano Espaillat
  • Claire Valdez (NY) won the primary for an open seat

These victories sent shockwaves through the Democratic establishment. House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries had personally campaigned for the incumbents being ousted. The Congressional Black Caucus PAC issued statements criticizing DSA as insufficiently focused on Black politics (citing a 2021 DSA survey showing 82% membership was white).

Key Question for Discussion

The Democratic establishment's response to the 2026 primary victories was hostile—Jeffries backed incumbents; the CBC criticized DSA candidates. Yet all three DSA winners will almost certainly win their general elections (their districts are 75%+ Democratic). Does the establishment's resistance in primaries matter if DSA wins anyway?

Nationally, the DSA endorsed approximately ~150 candidates across the 2026 midterms, and ~35 won. While this represents a real breakthrough, it masks an important limitation: nearly all DSA victories came in safe Democratic seats. In competitive districts, establishment-backed centrist candidates outperformed DSA challengers.

Party Response: Three Eras of Establishment Strategy

Era 1: Fusion and Rejection (1890s–1920s)

William Jennings Bryan's Populist-Democratic fusion (1896) demonstrated that the Democratic Party was willing to absorb left-wing demands when they were electorally powerful. However, by the 1920s, as industrial capital gained dominance over agrarian interests, the party jettisoned Populism. The Democratic Party of the 1920s was explicitly pro-business and anti-socialist.

Era 2: Suppression (1945–1990)

The Cold War created an alignment between the Democratic establishment and anti-communist labor unions. Socialists were purged from unions and party committees. The SPA, by the 1950s, was so marginalized that it ceased to be a political force. McCarthyism provided the mechanism: association with socialism could destroy a political career.

Era 3: Selective Absorption (2016–2024)

The Sanders campaigns and AOC's election forced the Democratic Party to reckon with democratic socialism's growing appeal. The party's response was strategic: accept the rhetoric, water down the policy, maintain control of the party apparatus.

On healthcare: The 2016 and 2020 DNC platforms rejected Medicare for All, settling instead on "healthcare is a right" language and ACA expansion. The 2024 Harris campaign explicitly rejected Medicare for All, framing it as politically unrealistic.

On climate: The Green New Deal, championed by AOC and DSA members, was incorporated into Democratic platform rhetoric. Yet Biden's Inflation Reduction Act, while significant ($369 billion in climate spending), relied on market-based incentives rather than the systemic decarbonization that socialists demanded.

On student debt: Biden attempted $10,000–$20,000 in student debt forgiveness, a policy that emerged from progressive pressure. Yet the courts blocked the program, and Biden did not pursue litigation to overturn the blockade—suggesting the policy was never a true priority.

On labor and worker power: While Democrats have adopted pro-union rhetoric, there has been no structural change to capital-labor relations. A federal jobs guarantee—a 1912 SPA demand that remains a core DSA platform position—has not been adopted.

The Pattern Repeats

Just as the Democratic Party absorbed New Deal language while rejecting socialist economics in the 1930s, contemporary Democrats adopt green language while rejecting the systemic transformation that would halt capital accumulation. The question becomes: At what point does absorption become co-optation so complete that DSA electoral victories become window-dressing on an unaltered capitalist state?

A Comparative Question: Why Did DSA Survive When the SPA Collapsed?

The Socialist Party reached 118,000 members in 1912 and effectively vanished by the 1950s. The DSA reached 120,000 members in 2026 and appears to be growing. Why the different trajectories?

1. Strategy: The SPA ran third-party presidential campaigns, which cost it Democratic Party support and invited suppression. The DSA works within Democratic primaries, remaining electorally viable regardless of party establishment hostility.

2. Structural Advantages: The internet and social media enable DSA recruitment and coordination in ways unavailable to the SPA. Digital organizing costs far less than printing newspapers and organizing unions. AOC's Twitter following is larger than the SPA's entire membership circa 2000.

3. Labor Decline: The SPA was rooted in trade unions. Union decline (from 33% of workforce in 1950 to 10.5% in 2024) eliminated that structural base—but it also removed the mechanism through which the Democratic Party could suppress socialism (union leaders served as gatekeepers). Today's DSA is rooted in precarious service workers, students, and intellectuals rather than organized labor.

4. Generational Factors: Younger Americans (under 35) show significantly higher support for socialism than older generations. A 2026 survey found 58% of Democrats view socialism favorably. This generational shift creates a persistent recruitment base unavailable to the SPA in the 1950s, when Cold War anti-communism was hegemonic.

5. No Competing Communist Party: The SPA was decimated partly because the Communist Party USA, aligned with the Soviet Union, offered a more revolutionary alternative. The CPUSA absorbed the most radical SPA members. Today, no such competitor exists. The DSA is the only significant American socialist organization.

But Is It Sustainable?

Growth alone does not guarantee durability. Several risks could undermine DSA's momentum:

  • Burnout: Rapid organizational growth often leads to internal conflict and member burnout. DSA has already experienced internal tensions over foreign policy (the 2024 DSA decision to withdraw endorsement from AOC over her Israel-Gaza position) and racial composition (82% white membership drawing criticism).
  • Democratic Party Co-optation: The more DSA victories normalize socialism within Democratic discourse, the more the party establishment can safely ignore DSA demands. This happened to unions: as union-backed Democrats gained power, they often abandoned pro-labor legislation.
  • Electoral Limits in Competitive Districts: DSA has won in safe Democratic seats. Scaling to actually win competitive races and change House control requires different coalitions and likely political compromises incompatible with DSA's base.
  • Age and Lifecycle: If current DSA members follow typical political trajectories, membership will decline as members age, face competing demands (jobs, family), and lose activist urgency. The 1990s DSA experienced this exact pattern.

Conclusion: The Eternal Return

The history of democratic socialism in America follows a recurring arc: emergence from economic crisis and labor struggle; rapid growth and organizational success; establishment counter-move through co-optation or suppression; decline or transformation into something smaller and less revolutionary.

The Populists were absorbed into Bryan's Democratic campaigns, then abandoned. The Socialist Party won 1,200 local offices, then saw them eliminated by Red Scare suppression and party division. DSA is now winning House primaries and mayoral races—real, tangible electoral power. Yet the question looms: Will this be co-opted like the New Deal absorbed socialist demands, or will DSA prove durable?

The data suggest grounds for both optimism and skepticism. Optimism: 120,000 members; generational support for socialism; electoral victories; mainstream platform shift toward some DSA positions. Skepticism: victories concentrated in safe seats; demographic composition (82% white) alienating potential allies; internal tensions over foreign policy; Democratic establishment still resisting key DSA demands (Medicare for All, federal jobs guarantee, labor organizing rights).

What seems certain is this: The DSA has forced American progressivism to reckon with questions of economic democracy, workers' power, and systemic inequality in ways unthinkable in 1980. Whether that reckonings leads to transformation or absorption remains the question that your group discussion can explore.

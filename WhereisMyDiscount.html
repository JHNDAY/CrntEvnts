“Where’s My Discount?”

Big law firms are racing to show off their AI tools. Now their clients want to know when all that efficiency will show up on the bill.

By Sarah Kessler · Visual summary of The New York Times DealBook newsletter

Outsiders often name law as an industry threatened by artificial intelligence. Yet the nation's biggest firms are eager to show off how they have embraced it. After OpenAI released a version of its latest model for lawyers, several top-tier firms unveiled tools of their own, following a year in which Google and Anthropic also launched legal AI products and firms began hiring chief AI officers.

For most businesses, a reputation for AI prowess would be pure good news. In an industry that bills by the hour, it raises an awkward question. If the work is faster, why isn't the bill smaller?

That’s great that you’re using A.I. … What does that mean for us?What clients are asking, per Neill Jakobe · Vice Chairman, Ropes & Gray

Some clients now use AI agents to comb their legal bills, flagging line items where AI could have saved hours and striking them out. Others point to a firm's own press release announcing a new AI tool. In-house legal teams are often asking for 20 to 30 percent off the bill across the board.

Firms push back. They say AI is not yet saving that much time, and that it is changing the mix of hours, with less busy work and more substantive analysis.

Inside the Story

Three forces on the billable hour

I · The Clients

AI Audits the Invoice

Clients are turning AI on their own bills. Agents flag line items where a firm's tools could have saved time, and clients strike them out.

Firms answer that AI is changing what the hours are spent on more than how many there are.

"I want a discount on this, we don't want to pay for this part of the work anymore."Clients' demands, as described by Jennifer Leonard, Creative Lawyers
II · The Competition

The Firm's Dilemma

AI-native law firms lean on AI agents and quote prices upfront. Norm AI now counts its own investors, Khosla Ventures, Blackstone and Bain Capital, among its clients.

Using AI cuts billable hours, and rates can't rise fast enough to make up the difference. Not using it invites rivals to undercut you.

"I don't think the billable hour is the solution, and we've known it for a long time."Jeff Bleich, international special envoy, Anthropic
III · The Future

A Slow, Split Shift

The billable hour runs through everything from insurance to performance reviews, and it protects firms on unpredictable matters. Few expect it to vanish.

Ropes & Gray now walks clients through where AI was used on each project and is more open to fixed fees. Kirkland's chairman says fixed-price work is already growing.

"Nobody wants to be the first firm who really leads the way and goes out there."Kyle Poe, Legora

Questions for Discussion

  1. Who Keeps the Savings?

    If a firm's AI does in minutes what once took a week, should the client get the savings, the firm, or both? Is a 20 to 30 percent discount a fair ask?

  2. Paying for Time or Value?

    The billable hour rewards slowness but protects firms on unpredictable work. Have you ever paid a flat fee for legal, medical or financial help? Which arrangement felt fairer to you?

  3. Nobody Wants to Go First

    Firms are prepared to change but are waiting for someone else to move. Where else have you seen an industry hold onto an old model until an outsider forced the change?

  4. Watching the Bill with AI

    Clients now use AI to audit their legal invoices. Would you use AI to check a bill from your lawyer, contractor or hospital? What could go wrong if both sides rely on machines to argue over hours?

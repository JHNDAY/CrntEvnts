The twilight of an autocratic age

These autocrats may seem immovable. But their time is passing — and none of them has a plan for what comes next.

At a Glance

Ignatius's core argument: the "age of the autocrats" looks dominant but is actually ending — each leader is aging, error-prone and has built no succession plan.

Costly Miscalculations

  • Putin's 2022 Ukraine invasion has cost Russia 1.4 million dead and wounded.
  • Trump's February attack on Iran has cost roughly $43 billion and hurt his political base.
  • Xi has purged 100+ senior military officers since 2023, ousting 5 of 7 Central Military Commission members.

Where They've Been Pushed Back

  • Trump's Greenland annexation threat galvanized European resistance and ended in a compromise Arctic-base deal.
  • Japan's Takaichi and the Philippines' Marcos both held firm against Chinese pressure.
  • Cheap drones — "weapons of the weak" — shifted the balance in both Ukraine and Iran.

Health & Succession

  • Xi missed a New Delhi banquet this month due to illness.
  • Putin is described as "in denial of his advanced age"; Trump shows his age at 80.
  • None of the three has cultivated a clear successor.

Who's Rising Instead

  • "Middle powers" are filling the space — led by Canada's Mark Carney, who rallied resistance to Trump's tariffs.

Ignatius opens with the conventional wisdom: Trump, Xi and Putin loom over the globe right now, with this week's U.N. session cast as a footnote to the real business happening at the Trump-Xi summit. His column argues the opposite is closer to true — that we are watching these three strongmen decline, not peak.

His case rests on a pattern he calls "autocrats' disease": each man's overconfidence has produced a costly unforced error. Putin's 2022 invasion of Ukraine, launched on the assumption of a quick win, has instead dragged Russia into an enormous casualty count. Trump's February strike on Iran, premised on the country being an easy target, has proven expensive and politically damaging at home. Ignatius reaches for a poker metaphor for Trump's continued threats against Iran at the U.N.: he's a player "on tilt," wrongly convinced his hand is unbeatable.

Xi gets a subtler version of the same critique. Rather than military overreach, his mistakes show up as internal distrust — a sustained purge of senior military leadership and periodic crackdowns on China's tech elite, even as he later walks some of them back.

The piece traces the "strongman era" framing to a 2025 remark by the late MI6 chief Alex Younger, who compared today's dynamic to the three-power carve-up at Yalta in 1945. Ignatius pushes back on that comparison: in practice, he writes, the big powers' attempts to bully smaller ones have mostly backfired. Cheap drones reshaped the fighting in both Ukraine and Iran, and pressure campaigns against Greenland, Japan and the Philippines all ended in the autocrats backing down or being held to a standstill rather than getting their way.

He's "on tilt" — the poker term for someone who mistakenly believes he holds an unbeatable hand.Ignatius, describing Trump's U.N. rhetoric toward Iran

Age and health are the column's closing argument. All three leaders are showing it — through public appearances, reported habits, or simply the calendar — and, more importantly, none has built a credible succession plan. Ignatius borrows a line from Warren Buffett's retirement announcement to make the point.

Father Time always wins.Warren Buffett, 96, quoted on his Berkshire Hathaway retirement

Rather than naming a single successor to the autocratic order, Ignatius points to "middle powers" as the beneficiaries of this drift, singling out Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney's resistance to Trump's tariffs as an early example of the pattern. He closes on a note of caution rather than triumph: these three men could stay dominant for years yet, China's rise doesn't depend on Xi personally, and the unresolved variable hanging over all of it — AI's transformational effect on power — remains genuinely unknown.

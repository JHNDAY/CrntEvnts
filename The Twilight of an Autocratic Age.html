The Twilight of an Autocratic Age

Is the strongman era peaking, or already ending? A facilitator's guide to David Ignatius's column.

Source: Washington Post Opinion, Sept. 22, 2026
The column in one sentence

Trump, Xi and Putin look dominant, but each is aging, prone to costly mistakes, and has no successor, so the "age of the autocrats" may already be ending.

Opening: peak or decline?

Set the scene
  • Conventional wisdom holds that three strongmen now run the world. Ignatius notes that this week's U.N. General Assembly was treated as a footnote, while the real attention went to the Trump–Xi summit.
  • His contrarian claim is that we are watching these leaders decline rather than peak. Their power looks unshakable from outside, he argues, but the foundations underneath it are weakening in ways that are easy to miss.
  • Their ages: Trump 80, Xi 73, Putin 73. Xi has led China since 2012 and Putin has run Russia, as president or prime minister, since 1999. None has publicly named a successor.
Warm-up poll (hands up or chat a number)
  1. The strongman era is still rising.
  2. It's at its peak.
  3. It's already in decline.
  4. Too soon to say.

"Autocrats' disease": costly unforced errors

What Ignatius says
  • Putin: invaded Ukraine in February 2022 expecting Kyiv to fall within days. More than four years later the war goes on, and Ignatius cites about 1.4 million Russian dead and wounded.
  • Trump: his February strike on Iran assumed the regime would fold quickly. Ignatius puts the cost at about $43 billion, says it hurt Trump with his base, and calls him a poker player "on tilt."
  • Xi: his errors are quieter. Since 2023 he has purged 100+ senior officers, including 5 of 7 members of the Central Military Commission, which commands China's armed forces. He also cracked down on tech leaders, then partly reversed course.

Is overconfidence a built-in flaw of one-man rule, or just bad luck in a few big bets?Who in these systems is able to tell the leader "no"?

Is it fair to group Trump with Xi and Putin? He faces elections, courts and a free press.

Xi's purges: a sign of weakness and distrust, or of a leader firmly in control?

The other side Democracies make costly mistakes too (Iraq 2003, Afghanistan). Is "autocrats' disease" really specific to autocrats?

Bullying smaller countries has backfired

What Ignatius says
  • In 2025 the late MI6 chief Alex Younger compared today's world to Yalta in 1945, when Roosevelt, Churchill and Stalin divided postwar Europe into spheres of influence. Ignatius argues the comparison doesn't fit what is actually happening.
  • Greenland: Trump's threat to annex Greenland, a self-governing territory of Denmark, alarmed European allies and pulled them together in resistance. Ignatius says it ended not in annexation but in a compromise deal over U.S. Arctic bases.
  • Japan & the Philippines: China has applied heavy diplomatic and maritime pressure on both countries. Japan's Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi and Philippine President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. held firm, and Beijing did not get the retreat it wanted.
  • Drones: cheap drones, which Ignatius calls "weapons of the weak," let Ukraine strike Russian ships, refineries and airbases, and shaped the fighting with Iran too. Inexpensive technology is offsetting much of the advantage that big militaries once enjoyed.

Is the Yalta comparison useful, or misleading?What made Yalta possible that is missing today?

Did Greenland end as a defeat for Trump, or did he get most of what he wanted?

Do cheap drones permanently shift power toward smaller nations, or will big powers adapt?

The other side Holding firm is not the same as winning. Russia still occupies Ukrainian territory; China's pressure on its neighbors continues.

Father Time and the succession problem

What Ignatius says
  • Xi missed a banquet in New Delhi in September because of illness, a rare public absence. In China's tightly controlled system, even small signs about the leader's health draw intense attention from analysts and foreign governments.
  • Ignatius describes Putin as in denial about his advanced age. He says Trump, who was the oldest person ever inaugurated as president, is now visibly showing his years at 80 in public appearances.
  • None of the three has groomed a successor. Ignatius borrows a line from Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway retirement announcement, "Father Time always wins," to argue that every strongman eventually runs into this problem.

Why do strongmen so rarely name a successor?Does naming one create a rival?

For the U.S., succession is set by the Constitution. Is the "no successor" problem the same for Trump as for Xi and Putin?

Historically, what happens when an aging autocrat dies without a plan? Think Stalin, Franco, Mao.

Facilitator note Keep this on leadership and institutions, not personal health speculation.

Who rises instead?

What Ignatius says
  • Ignatius sees "middle powers" filling the space the big three leave. His main example is Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney, who rallied resistance to Trump's tariffs and encouraged mid-sized democracies to stand together.
  • He hedges his own argument. These three leaders could stay dominant for years, he writes, and China's rise does not depend on Xi personally, so China's growing power would likely outlast him.
  • The great unknown, in his view, is artificial intelligence. Leadership in AI may bring large economic and military advantages, and no one yet knows whether it will strengthen autocrats or the democracies opposing them.

Can middle powers really coordinate, or will they be picked off one by one?

Does AI favor autocrats (surveillance, control) or their challengers (cheap tools, open information)?

The column ran Sept. 22. What has happened in the four weeks since that supports or undercuts it?

Wrap-up

Re-poll

Same four choices as the opening. Did anyone change their answer? Ask one person who moved to say why.

In one sentence: is Ignatius describing reality, or hoping for it?

Closing thought to read aloud

Ignatius ends with caution, not victory. Autocracies may be weaker than they look, but decline can take a long time, and a messy transition can be more dangerous than a stable strongman.
